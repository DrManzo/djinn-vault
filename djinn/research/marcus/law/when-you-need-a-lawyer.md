# When You Need a Lawyer

## The Core Principle

Lawyers are expensive — California small business attorneys typically charge $250–$500/hour, sometimes more in specialized fields. At the same time, trying to handle complex legal situations yourself can cost far more when things go wrong. The skill is knowing which situations are genuinely DIY-able and which carry enough risk that professional guidance pays for itself.

This document gives a clear framework for that decision, specifically calibrated to a one-person operation in California doing 3D printing, content creation, and cannabis-adjacent product sales.

---

## DIY-Able — Handle Yourself (with research and templates)

### Business Formation
- Filing Articles of Organization for a single-member LLC: $70 + your time
- Obtaining an EIN from the IRS: free, 5 minutes online
- Getting a CDTFA seller's permit: free, 20 minutes online
- Getting a city business license: usually $50–$150, standard form
- Writing a basic single-member LLC Operating Agreement: use a template, fill it in

### Standard Contracts
- Simple service agreements with individual clients (under $10k)
- Custom order agreements for 3D printed products
- Non-disclosure agreements with standard terms
- Basic terms and conditions for your website or shop
- Using free/cheap templates from Docracy, LegalZoom, or SCORE is fine for these

### Basic Research
- Understanding what law says (statutes, regulations)
- Reading contracts before signing
- Small claims court filings (under $12,500 for individuals)

### Dispute Resolution
- Demand letters for unpaid invoices (write them yourself)
- Small claims court: no lawyers allowed in court anyway; you represent yourself
- CDTFA, FTB, and IRS correspondence for routine matters (amended returns, basic audits)

## Use a Lawyer — Investment in Risk Reduction

### Situations That Justify an Attorney

**1. Multi-party LLC formation with co-founders**
When two or more people start a business together and one of them is bringing the primary IP, customer relationships, or capital — the Operating Agreement needs professional drafting. Equity splits that seem fair at $0 revenue feel different at $500k revenue. A well-drafted Operating Agreement costs $1,500–$3,000 and prevents lawsuits worth multiples of that.

**2. You are being sued, or served with a lawsuit**
This is not optional. Even if the claim is bogus, failing to respond to a civil lawsuit results in a **default judgment** against you — the plaintiff wins automatically. Once you have a default judgment against you, your assets can be levied. If you're served with a complaint, you typically have **30 days** in California to file a written response (CCP § 412.20).

**3. Significant contracts — over $25k or with unusual terms**
For large client contracts, licensing deals, or any agreement where the other side's attorney drafted the document, a single attorney review ($300–$600) can catch IP traps, one-sided indemnification, and unlimited liability exposure that self-review misses.

**4. Employment — the moment you hire your first employee**
California has some of the most complex employment law in the US: minimum wage tiers, meal break rules, paid sick leave, PAGA penalties, worker classification (AB5 for contractors). A single misclassified independent contractor can result in back taxes, penalties, and PAGA class-action exposure. Before hiring anyone, get an employment attorney consultation.

**5. Intellectual property — trademark registration**
Filing a trademark yourself is technically DIY-able but error-prone. A rejected application loses your filing fee ($250–$350/class) and delays protection by months. An IP attorney ($1,000–$2,000) greatly increases approval probability and correctly identifies the scope of protection.

**6. Tax disputes — IRS or FTB audit (formal)**
If the IRS or FTB initiates a formal audit, an Enrolled Agent (EA) or CPA handles routine audits; a tax attorney handles litigation, criminal investigations, or complex disputes. Don't go to a formal audit alone without professional representation.

**7. Cannabis-adjacent business — expansion or licensing**
If you move beyond accessories into any regulated cannabis space (dispensary, distributor, manufacturer), you need a California cannabis compliance attorney. DCC licensing has specific requirements, local approval processes, and serious penalties for non-compliance. This is not a DIY area.

**8. Criminal investigation**
If you receive notice of a criminal investigation, or are interviewed by law enforcement in connection with your business — stop talking and call a criminal defense attorney immediately. Fifth Amendment. Always.

## How to Find a Lawyer Without Overpaying

### Free and Low-Cost Resources

**California State Bar Lawyer Referral Service**
- https://www.calbar.ca.gov/public/lawyers/referral
- Connects you with attorneys in your area by practice area
- Many offer a free or reduced-cost initial consultation (30 minutes)

**San Bernardino County Bar Association**
- Lawyer referral service for San Bernardino residents
- Check: https://www.sbcba.org

**Legal Aid Society of San Bernardino**
- Free legal services for income-qualifying individuals
- Business formation and basic contract help

**SCORE (Service Corps of Retired Executives)**
- Free business mentoring from retired executives (many with legal backgrounds)
- SBA-affiliated: https://www.score.org
- Not legal advice, but excellent for business structure guidance and contract templates

**California Small Business Development Centers (SBDC)**
- Free consulting on business formation, compliance, and growth
- Inland Empire SBDC: https://iesmallbusiness.com

**Law School Clinics**
- Chapman University, UC Riverside, and other regional law schools run clinics
- Law students supervised by attorneys provide free services for qualifying situations

**Avvo / Martindale-Hubbell**
- Attorney directories with reviews and ratings
- Many attorneys post free Q&A responses — useful for quick guidance (not a substitute for representation)

### When Talking to an Attorney
1. **Prepare first**: have a clear 2-minute summary of your situation, what you want to accomplish, and what you've already done
2. **Ask about fee structure upfront**: hourly, flat fee, or contingency? Ask for a written fee agreement (California Business & Professions Code § 6148 requires written fee agreements for fees over $1,000 in most cases)
3. **Ask for a clear scope of engagement**: what exactly will they do for the money?
4. **Don't sign the retainer on the spot**: take it home, read it
5. **Ask if there's a flat-fee option for your specific need**: many attorneys offer flat fees for formation, contract review, trademark filing

## The "Letter-of-the-Law vs. Legal Judgment" Distinction

Reading and understanding the law (what it says) is different from applying legal judgment (what it means for your specific situation). The first is increasingly DIY-able with good research skills. The second is what attorneys sell.

Example:
- You can read Cal. Labor Code § 2775 (AB5) yourself and understand the ABC test for contractor classification
- Determining whether YOUR specific working arrangement with a specific person satisfies that test — given the facts of your relationship — is where legal judgment matters and mistakes are costly

The practical rule: **read the law yourself to understand the landscape, hire an attorney for the application to your specific facts when stakes are high.**
