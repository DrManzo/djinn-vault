Law school in the United States operates on a single standard path: earn a bachelor's degree in any field, sit for the LSAT, and apply through LSAC. ## 2. LSAT Preparation — Deep Strategy

The LSAT is the most important single number in law school admissions. A 170+ opens essentially every school. A 165 is competitive at T25. A 155 places you at the national median. The test rewards **trained reasoning skills, not innate intelligence** — the research confirms this emphatically.[^4]

### Structure (2026 Format)[^5][^6]

The current LSAT is administered digitally through LawHub, LSAC's testing platform. It consists of:


| Section | Questions | Time | Weight |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Logical Reasoning (LR)** | ~26 questions | 35 min | **~50% of scored questions** |
| **Analytical Reasoning (AR / Logic Games)** | ~23 questions | 35 min | ~25% |
| **Reading Comprehension (RC)** | ~27 questions | 35 min | ~25% |
| **Unscored Variable Section** | Identical to above | 35 min | 0% — cannot be identified |
| **Writing Sample** | 1 argument | No time limit | Submitted separately; reviewed by schools but unscored |

**Score range:** 120–180. National median: approximately 152. T14 practical floor: 170+.[^1]

### Section-by-Section Deep Dive

#### Logical Reasoning — Where Most Points Are Won and Lost

LR comprises approximately 50% of your scored questions.  Every question presents a stimulus (a short argument or set of statements) and a question stem asking you to do something specific with it.[^7]

**Core argument anatomy — you must understand this cold:**

- **Conclusion**: the claim the argument is trying to establish. Often flagged by: therefore, thus, hence, so, as a result, it follows that, clearly, obviously, must be
- **Premise**: evidence offered to support the conclusion. Often flagged by: because, since, given that, for, as evidenced by
- **Assumption**: the unstated belief the argument requires to be valid — the logical gap between premises and conclusion
- **Flaw**: the logical error the argument commits (if any)

**LR Question Types — Complete Map:**[^8][^7]


| Question Type | What It Asks | Core Skill |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Must Be True / Most Strongly Supported** | What must be true given the statements? | Pure inference from stated content only |
| **Main Point / Conclusion** | What is the argument's conclusion? | Identifying the logical endpoint |
| **Assumption (Necessary)** | What must the argument assume? | Finding the logical gap — the negation test: if the assumption is false, does the argument collapse? |
| **Assumption (Sufficient)** | What would, if added, guarantee the conclusion? | Bridging premises to conclusion |
| **Strengthen** | What makes the argument more compelling? | Adding support to the logical structure |
| **Weaken** | What undermines the argument? | Attacking the assumption or introducing a competing explanation |
| **Flaw** | What is wrong with this reasoning? | Naming the logical error (circular reasoning, sampling error, ad hominem, false dichotomy, confusing correlation/causation, etc.) |
| **Parallel Reasoning** | Which choice has the same logical structure? | Abstract pattern matching, not content |
| **Point at Issue / Disagree** | What do these two speakers disagree about? | Finding the exact disputed proposition |
| **Principle (Apply/Identify)** | Which principle matches this situation? | Pattern matching between abstract rule and concrete case |
| **Explain / Resolve Paradox** | What reconciles these apparently contradictory facts? | Causal explanation |
| **Evaluate** | What information would be most useful to assess the argument? | Identifying pivotal variables |
| **Complete the Argument** | What logically completes this passage? | Inference + direction |

**The Negation Test** (for Necessary Assumption questions): Take your candidate assumption and negate it. If the negation destroys the argument — makes the conclusion impossible or irrelevant — you've found the necessary assumption. This is the most reliable mechanical test in all of LR.[^8]

**Conditional Reasoning** is the underlying logic structure of dozens of LR and all AR questions. Master this:

- "If A, then B" means A → B (A is sufficient for B; B is necessary for A)
- Contrapositive: NOT B → NOT A (logically equivalent; equally valid)
- Converse (B → A) and inverse (NOT A → NOT B) are **NOT** logically equivalent — treating them as such is the most common LR error


#### Analytical Reasoning (Logic Games) — Most Learnable Section[^9][^8]

Logic Games presents 4 game scenarios per section, each with 5–7 questions. Each game describes an ordering, grouping, matching, or combination arrangement with a set of rules, and asks you to determine what arrangements are possible, impossible, or required.

**Why this section is most learnable:** It is a pure rule-application mechanical skill. With sufficient exposure to the game types and mastery of diagramming, near-perfect scores are achievable for most students regardless of their starting level.[^9]

**The Four Game Types:**

1. **Sequencing/Ordering**: Place items in a linear sequence (e.g., seven books on a shelf, left to right). Most common type. Diagram: a numbered left-to-right row with slots.
2. **Grouping**: Assign items to two or more groups (e.g., committee assignments). Diagram: labeled boxes/columns.
3. **Matching**: Assign attributes to entities (e.g., each person wears one of three colors on each of two days). Grid diagram.
4. **Hybrid**: Combines sequencing + grouping (most complex; appears occasionally).

**Diagramming System** (7Sage / PowerScore approach):[^8]

- Create a master diagram that represents the constraints
- Use letters for entities (A, B, C) and numbers for positions (1, 2, 3)
- Represent conditional rules as: A → B, ¬B → ¬A
- Represent "not with" rules as: A ≠ B
- Represent "before" rules as: A–B (A precedes B)
- After diagramming all rules, **chain them together** to generate inferences before looking at questions. The best LG test-takers spend 2–3 minutes building the master diagram + initial inferences, then answer 5–7 questions in 4–6 minutes.

**Common game archetypes:**

- **Stable games**: Same rules apply to every question (most)
- **"If" questions**: Introduce a hypothetical constraint for that question only — make a temporary diagram, don't alter your master
- **"Which could be true" questions**: Eliminate answers that violate rules; use process of elimination
- **"What must be true" questions**: Must be true in ALL valid arrangements — check against your inferences first

**Practice protocol for AR:** 7Sage's Logic Games course is widely considered the gold standard for this section.  Target: drilling 4+ games per day after the initial learning phase, until you complete every game in under 8 minutes with 100% accuracy.[^6]

#### Reading Comprehension — The Underestimated Section[^8]

RC is 4 passages per section, each 450–600 words, with 5–8 questions each. Passages come from natural science, social science, law, and humanities. One passage is always a "comparative reading" set — two shorter passages on the same topic that you compare and contrast.

**The LSAT RC trap:** Many test-takers approach it as a general reading test. It is not. It is a **precise logical argument analysis test using passage content**. Every question has one defensibly correct answer that is directly supported by the passage. No outside knowledge is needed or helpful — using it actively hurts you.

**Active Reading Protocol for LSAT RC:**[^8]

1. **Preview**: Read the first sentence of each paragraph before reading the whole passage (~15 seconds). What is the overarching structure?
2. **Read for structure, not details**: Your goal is to understand:
    - What is the main point/thesis?
    - What is the author's attitude/tone toward the subject?
    - What does each paragraph DO (introduce, counter, support, qualify, conclude)?
    - How do the paragraphs relate to each other logically?
3. **Mark minimally**: Note paragraph purpose and location of specific topics (you'll return to the passage for specific details — don't memorize)
4. **Predict before reading answer choices**: For inference and main point questions, formulate your own answer before looking at the choices. Prediction dramatically improves accuracy.

**RC Question Types:**

- **Main Point**: The central claim of the entire passage — not a detail, not one paragraph's argument
- **Author's Attitude/Tone**: What does the author think? Look for evaluative language (fortunately, unfortunately, surprisingly, mistakenly, correctly)
- **Specific Detail**: Re-read the relevant passage section precisely — paraphrase the relevant text
- **Inference**: What must be true based on what the passage states? Stay within the passage; do not extrapolate
- **Function/Purpose**: Why did the author include this paragraph or example? What role does it play?
- **Comparative Reading**: Which statement would both/neither/one author agree with?


### Practice Test Strategy[^9][^8]

**The most common mistake in LSAT prep:** Doing volume without deep review. Doing 20 practice tests with no written analysis of missed questions produces far less improvement than doing 8 tests with complete question-by-question blind review.

**The Three-Phase Practice Protocol:**

1. **Untimed drilling**: First learn the question types fully without time pressure. Accuracy without speed.
2. **Timed drilling**: Section-by-section under full 35-minute constraint. Identify your accuracy/speed tradeoff.
3. **Full PrepTests under realistic conditions**: 4 sections, 35 minutes each, no breaks between sections, no phone, quiet environment — exactly as you'll encounter on test day.

**Blind Review Method** (most important study technique): After completing a PrepTest:

- Mark every question you were uncertain about (even if you got it right)
- Before looking at the answers, re-work every flagged question without time pressure
- Compare your timed answer vs. your blind-review answer vs. the correct answer
- Every discrepancy is data: why did time pressure produce the wrong answer? what reasoning error was made?

**Volume targets for strong improvement:**[^9][^8]

- Minimum: 10–15 full PrepTests
- Target: 20+ PrepTests for 170+ aspirants
- Logic Games: 200+ individual games drilled to fluency
- LR: 500+ individual questions with written explanations for every missed question

**Resources rated for 2026:**[^6]

- **Khan Academy + LawHub (Free)**: Official LSAT prep from LSAC. All PrepTests from PT 1–88 available on LawHub. The free tier covers the foundation.
- **7Sage** (~\$170–\$400): Superior for Logic Games instruction (J.Y. Ping's video explanations are exceptional). Also strong for LR.
- **PowerScore Bibles** (~\$45/each for LR, LG, RC books): The conceptual foundation — deep, systematic, principle-based. Best for building the underlying framework.

### Essay Writing Strategy — IRAC and Beyond[^14][^13]

The California bar essays require **organized, law-applied writing under time pressure**. Examiners read hundreds of essays. They reward clarity, legal precision, and direct application — not eloquence or comprehensiveness.

**IRAC Framework (Issue → Rule → Application → Conclusion):**

```
ISSUE:     Identify the precise legal question raised by the facts.
           "The issue is whether [specific party] may [specific claim/defense] under [doctrine]."

RULE:      State the governing rule with all elements.
           "Under California law, [doctrine] requires: (1) _____, (2) _____, (3) _____, (4) _____."

APPLICATION: Apply each element to the specific facts. This is where most students lose points.
             Don't just recite facts. Don't just recite law. Connect each fact to each element.
             "Here, element (1) is met because [specific fact from the prompt]..."
             "Element (3) is arguable because [competing interpretations of the facts]..."

CONCLUSION: State the legal conclusion for this issue.
            "Therefore, [party] [will/will not] prevail on [claim] because [one-sentence reason]."
```

**Common essay mistakes:**[^14]

- Stating the rule without applying it ("spot the issue and dump the law" — gets partial credit only)
- Writing one massive paragraph instead of separate, organized IRAC analyses for each issue
- Missing a sub-issue (many prompts have 3–5 issues; missing one costs significant partial credit)
- Writing about law not raised by the facts ("kitchen-sinking" — irrelevant law wastes time and reads poorly)
- Forgetting California-specific modifications when a CA subject is tested alongside a general common law rule

**Time allocation for Day 1:**[^14]

- 5 essays × 60 minutes = 300 minutes. Budget: 5–8 minutes reading/outlining + 52–55 minutes writing per essay
- PT: 90 minutes. Budget: 30–35 minutes reading the file and library + 55 minutes writing the document


### Performance Test (PT) — The Most Learnable Day 1 Component

The PT gives you: a File (facts — client memos, interview transcripts, pleadings, exhibits) and a Library (cases, statutes, regulations — the law applicable to the problem). You must produce a specified legal document (memo, brief, letter, declaration) within 90 minutes.[^14]

**What makes it learnable:** You are not tested on memorized law. You are tested on legal writing competence — the ability to identify relevant authorities from the library, extract the applicable rules, apply them to the facts from the file, and produce the correct document type in proper format.

**PT strategy:**[^14]

1. Read the task memo first (it tells you what document to write and who the audience is)
2. Skim the library table of contents to understand available authority
3. Read the file for relevant facts (not all file content is relevant)
4. Read the library to extract rules
5. Write the document in the format specified — if it says write an objective memo, the structure is: heading, issue statement, brief answer, discussion (IRAC), conclusion. Do NOT write an adversarial brief if you're asked for an objective memo.

## 4. Contracts — Full Doctrine

Contract law is the body of law governing voluntary agreements between parties. The two parallel systems in American contract law are **common law** (courts, primarily Restatement (Second) of Contracts) and the **Uniform Commercial Code Article 2** (statutory, goods transactions).[^15][^16]

### Formation — The Four Elements Dissected

**1. Mutual Assent — Offer + Acceptance**

An **offer** must be: (a) an expression of a willingness to enter a contract on specific terms, (b) communicated to the offeree, (c) creating a reasonable expectation in the offeree that acceptance is invited and will form a binding contract.[^16]

- **Advertisements** are generally NOT offers — they are invitations to make offers. Exception: "First come, first served" advertisements with specific, definite terms may constitute offers (*Lefkowitz v. Great Minneapolis Surplus Store*).
- An offer terminates by: rejection, counteroffer, lapse of stated time (or reasonable time), revocation by offeror (before acceptance), or destruction of subject matter.
- **Option contracts** prevent revocation for the option period if supported by consideration.

**Acceptance** under common law: Must be the **mirror image** of the offer — any modification is a rejection and counteroffer. Must be communicated (silence is not acceptance, with narrow exceptions). Must be made by the offeree (non-assignable unless offer indicates otherwise).

**The Mailbox Rule**: An acceptance is effective upon dispatch (dropping in the mailbox), not upon receipt — unless the offeror specifies receipt is required. Rejections and revocations are effective upon receipt. This creates a window where: rejection dispatched → revocation received before rejection received → acceptance dispatched after revocation but before rejection received = contract formed.

**UCC Article 2 Acceptance:** Under UCC §2-207 ("Battle of the Forms"), an acceptance that adds additional or different terms is still a valid acceptance (unlike common law). Between merchants, additional terms in the acceptance become part of the contract unless: (a) the offer expressly limits acceptance to its terms, (b) the offeror objects within a reasonable time, or (c) the additional terms materially alter the contract.[^15]

**2. Consideration**

Consideration requires: (a) a legal detriment to the promisee OR a legal benefit to the promisor, and (b) the detriment/benefit must be bargained-for — exchanged for the promise.[^16]

**What is NOT consideration:**

- **Past consideration**: An act already performed before the promise is made cannot be consideration for the promise (the past act was not bargained-for the new promise)
- **Pre-existing duty rule**: A promise to do what you're already legally obligated to do provides no consideration (hence why police officers can't demand extra payment for performing their duty). Exception: modification of a contract modifying an ongoing duty
- **Illusory promise**: A "promise" that doesn't actually commit the promisor to anything (e.g., "I'll buy your car if I feel like it")

**UCC modification**: Under UCC §2-209, a contract for goods can be modified **without new consideration** — just good faith and a signed writing if the original contract required a writing.

**Promissory Estoppel** as a substitute for consideration: When a promisor makes a promise the promisor should reasonably expect to induce action or forbearance by the promisee, and the promisee reasonably relies on it to their detriment, a court may enforce the promise to prevent injustice. The remedy may be limited to reliance damages rather than expectation damages.[^16]

**3. Capacity**

Minors (under 18) may **disaffirm** contracts at any time before or within a reasonable time after reaching majority — except contracts for necessities (food, shelter, clothing, medical care), which minors may disaffirm but must pay the reasonable value of. Mentally incapacitated persons and intoxicated persons may also disaffirm if incapacity was apparent to the other party.

**4. Legality**

Contracts for illegal purposes are void — not voidable, but void from the start. No enforcement, no damages. Includes: contracts to commit crimes or torts, contracts in restraint of trade (except to the extent enforceable under applicable law), gambling contracts in some states.

### Statute of Frauds — What Must Be in Writing[^17][^15]

The Statute of Frauds requires a writing signed by the party to be charged for:

- **Real property contracts** (sale or lease over 1 year)
- **Contracts for goods over \$500** (UCC §2-201)
- **Contracts not performable within 1 year** from contract formation
- **Marriage contracts** (prenuptials)
- **Surety contracts** (guaranty of another's debt)

Writing requirements: must identify parties, subject matter, essential terms, and be signed by the party asserting the defense. Multiple documents can together satisfy the requirement if they reference each other.

**UCC exceptions to the writing requirement:** Merchants' memo (if one merchant sends a written confirmation and the other doesn't object within 10 days), part performance (payment accepted or goods received and accepted), specially manufactured goods.

### Performance, Breach, and Conditions[^17][^15]

**Conditions precedent**: Events that must occur before a party's contractual duty arises. If the condition doesn't occur, the duty never arises — no breach. Distinguished from a promise, which creates an obligation; a condition creates a qualification on an obligation.

**Material breach**: A breach so significant that it discharges the non-breaching party's obligations and entitles them to sue for all damages. Factors: extent of performance rendered, adequacy of compensation in damages, likelihood of cure, extent of harm, good faith of breaching party.

**Minor breach (partial breach)**: Non-breaching party must continue to perform but can sue for damages from the breach.

**Anticipatory repudiation**: An unambiguous statement or action before the performance is due that the party will not perform. This immediately discharges the non-breaching party, who can sue immediately without waiting for the performance date.

### Remedies for Breach — Complete Map[^15][^17][^16]

**Expectation damages** (the default): Put the non-breaching party in the position they would have been in had the contract been fully performed. = Benefit of the bargain — any remaining value of performance they're not getting.

**Consequential damages**: Additional damages beyond the direct value of performance — downstream losses caused by the breach. Must be **foreseeable** at the time of contracting as a probable result of breach (*Hadley v. Baxendale* rule). If the breaching party didn't know about special circumstances that would make losses unusually large, those losses aren't recoverable.

**Reliance damages**: Restore the non-breaching party to the position they were in before the contract (reimbursement for expenses incurred in reliance on the contract). Used when expectation damages are too speculative to calculate.

**Restitution**: Recovery of the benefit conferred on the breaching party to prevent unjust enrichment. Available even in the absence of an enforceable contract.

**Specific performance**: Available when money damages are inadequate — typically for unique goods (one-of-a-kind items, real property, rare goods). Courts won't order specific performance of personal service contracts (slavery prohibition; courts also can't enforce them practically).

**Liquidated damages clauses**: Pre-agreed contractually stipulated damage amount. Enforceable if: (a) actual damages were difficult to estimate at time of contracting, and (b) the stipulated amount is a reasonable forecast, not a penalty. If the amount is disproportionate to likely harm, courts will void it as a penalty and award actual damages.

**Punitive damages: NOT AVAILABLE in contract law.** Punitive damages are a tort remedy. A breach of contract, however morally reprehensible, does not support punitive damages — only the plaintiff's economic loss. This is one of the most frequently tested distinctions in law school and bar exams.[^16]

**Duty to mitigate**: The non-breaching party has a duty to take reasonable steps to reduce their losses after breach. Cannot recover losses that could have been avoided with reasonable effort.

### Defenses to Contract Enforcement[^15][^16]

| Defense | Effect | Substance |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Fraud / Misrepresentation** | Voidable | Intentional or negligent false statement of material fact inducing assent |
| **Duress** | Voidable | Improper threat that leaves the party no reasonable alternative |
| **Undue Influence** | Voidable | Unfair persuasion exploiting a special relationship (elder, confidant, fiduciary) |
| **Mutual Mistake** | Voidable | Both parties mistaken about a material fact at formation |
| **Unilateral Mistake** | Voidable (narrow) | One party mistaken; relief only if other party knew or should have known |
| **Unconscionability** | Void | Shockingly unfair in procedural terms (oppression, surprise) AND substantive terms (grossly one-sided) |
| **Impossibility** | Discharged | Performance becomes objectively impossible (subject matter destroyed, key person dies) |
| **Impracticability** | Discharged (modern) | Performance technically possible but commercially unreasonable due to unforeseen event |
| **Frustration of Purpose** | Discharged | Event destroys the value/purpose of the contract though performance is still technically possible |

## 5. Torts — Full Doctrine

Tort law provides civil remedies for civil wrongs — conduct causing harm to another person or their interests. The three categories are negligence, intentional torts, and strict liability.[^18][^12]

### Negligence — The Full Elements

**Element 1: Duty**

The defendant must have owed a legal duty of care to the plaintiff. General rule: everyone has a duty to exercise reasonable care toward foreseeable plaintiffs who might be harmed by their conduct.[^18]

**Special duty rules:**

- **Landowner duties (CA)**: California abolished the common law invitee/licensee/trespasser distinction (*Rowland v. Christian*, 1968) in favor of a general reasonable care standard for all entrants, with factors including probability of harm, burden to defendant, nature of risk, and social utility. Most states retain the traditional categories.
- **Professional duties**: Doctors, lawyers, engineers owe a duty based on the professional standard of care in their field — not the ordinary person standard.
- **No duty to rescue** (general common law): Absent a special relationship, no one has a legal duty to assist a stranger in danger. Special relationships that create a duty to aid: innkeeper-guest, employer-employee, carrier-passenger, common carriers, parent-child.
- **Negligent infliction of emotional distress (NIED)**: California recognizes NIED for bystanders who witness serious injury to a close family member in close proximity. Requirements: (1) close relationship, (2) physically present at the scene, (3) actually witnessed the event.

**Element 2: Breach**

Breach = failure to exercise the care a **reasonable prudent person (RPP)** would exercise under the same circumstances. The RPP standard is:

- Objective, not subjective — what would a reasonable person do, not what this particular defendant intended
- Contextually modified: a reasonable driver, a reasonable surgeon, a reasonable manufacturer

**The Hand Formula** (Judge Learned Hand, *United States v. Carroll Towing*, 1947): Negligence exists when B < P × L. B = burden of precaution, P = probability of harm, L = magnitude of harm. If the burden of preventing the harm is less than the expected harm, failure to take precaution is negligent.

**Res Ipsa Loquitur** ("the thing speaks for itself"): Allows an inference of negligence when: (1) the accident is of a type that ordinarily doesn't happen without negligence, (2) the defendant was in exclusive control of the instrumentality, (3) the plaintiff was not contributorily negligent. Classic example: surgical sponge left in a patient's abdomen.

**Negligence per se**: When a defendant violates a statute designed to protect against the type of harm that occurred, and the plaintiff is in the class of persons the statute protects, the violation constitutes negligence as a matter of law (breach is established without further analysis).

**Element 3: Causation — Actual Cause**

Two tests:

- **But-for causation**: "But for the defendant's negligence, would the plaintiff's injury have occurred?" Standard test.
- **Substantial factor test**: When multiple defendants' negligence each would independently have caused the same harm, each defendant is a substantial factor in causing the harm (both liable even though neither was strictly "but-for").

**Element 4: Proximate Cause**

Legal cause — limiting liability to foreseeable consequences of the negligent act. The harm must be a **foreseeable result** of the breach. The precise manner of harm doesn't need to be foreseeable, but the general type of harm must be.

**Superseding cause**: An intervening act that breaks the chain of causation — makes the original defendant's negligence not the proximate cause. Criteria: unforeseeable and extraordinary, not a natural or probable result of the negligent situation.

**Thin skull rule (Eggshell Plaintiff)**: You take your victim as you find them. If the plaintiff had a pre-existing vulnerability that made the harm far worse, you are fully liable for the full extent of harm — even if it was not foreseeable that a normal person would have suffered so much.

**Element 5: Damages**

Plaintiff must prove actual damages — personal injury, property damage, economic loss. Unlike some intentional torts, negligence without provable damages = no recovery (nominal damages not available).

**Comparative Fault (California)**: California uses **pure comparative negligence** (*Li v. Yellow Cab Co.*, 1975). The plaintiff's damages are reduced by their percentage of fault. A 99% at-fault plaintiff can still recover 1% of their damages. Most states use modified comparative fault (bars recovery if plaintiff's fault exceeds 50% or 51%).

### 2025 California Personal Injury Law Reforms[^18]

California enacted significant changes in 2025 to personal injury law:

- **Updated economic damages calculation**: Reforms to how medical damages are calculated, moving toward actual negotiated rates rather than billed charges in some circumstances
- **Medical provider billing changes**: Limits on evidence of inflated medical bills vs. amounts actually paid or payable


### Intentional Torts — Complete Map[^12][^18]

Intent for intentional torts = either (a) **purpose** to bring about the harmful consequence, OR (b) **substantial certainty** that the harmful consequence will result from the act. Motive is irrelevant — intending a harmful contact in jest is still battery.

**Battery**: An **intentional** act causing a harmful or offensive contact with another's person without consent. Key elements:

- Contact: can be indirect (throwing something at someone, touching their clothing, moving something in contact with them)
- Harmful or offensive: offensive to a **reasonable person** of ordinary sensibilities (the hypersensitive plaintiff gets no extra recovery)
- Without consent (express or implied): contact within the normal range of social interaction has implied consent (a gentle tap on the shoulder to get attention)
- Intent to contact is sufficient — don't need intent to harm

**Assault**: An **intentional** act creating a **reasonable apprehension** of imminent harmful or offensive contact in the plaintiff's mind. Key elements:

- Words alone generally insufficient — needs some accompanying act
- Plaintiff must be aware of the threatened contact at the time (no apprehension = no assault)
- "Apprehension" ≠ "fear" — apprehension means awareness/anticipation of contact, not necessarily terror. A capable martial artist can be assaulted by a weaker person who doesn't scare them.
- Imminence required — "I'll get you next week" ≠ assault; "I'm going to hit you right now" while raising a fist = assault

**False Imprisonment**: **Intentional** act confining plaintiff within fixed boundaries without consent or legal authority. Key elements:

- Confinement must be complete (blocking one exit when others are available is not FI)
- Plaintiff must know of the confinement or be harmed by it
- Moral pressure alone is insufficient — must be physical barriers, physical force, or threat of force
- Shopkeeper's privilege: reasonable detention for reasonable time for reasonable cause to investigate shoplifting (affirmative defense)

**IIED (Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress)**: **Intentional or reckless** extreme and outrageous conduct causing severe emotional distress. Key elements:

- "Extreme and outrageous": conduct that exceeds all bounds of decency tolerated by a civilized society ("a reasonable person would exclaim 'outrageous!'")
- Causation: the distress must be caused by the outrageous conduct
- "Severe": not just upset or embarrassed — must be significant emotional damage
- California and many states recognize third-party IIED: someone who witnesses outrageous conduct directed at a close family member in their presence can recover

**Defamation**: A false statement of fact (not opinion) published to a third party without privilege, causing damages to the plaintiff's reputation.[^12]

**Libel vs. Slander:**

- Libel = written, recorded, or broadcast defamation. Damages presumed in many states (per se).
- Slander = spoken defamation. Must prove actual damages unless slander per se (accusations of crime, loathsome disease, professional misconduct, sexual immorality).

**Actual malice standard** (*New York Times v. Sullivan*, 1964): Public figures and public officials must prove actual malice (knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard for truth) to recover in defamation. This is a First Amendment constitutional protection.

**Privacy Torts (separate category):**

- Intrusion upon seclusion
- Public disclosure of private facts
- False light
- Appropriation of name or likeness


### Strict Liability — When Fault Is Irrelevant[^19][^20][^18]

**Abnormally dangerous activities**: Strict liability for ultrahazardous activities creating a high degree of risk that cannot be adequately prevented through reasonable care. Criteria: high probability of harm, seriousness of potential harm, inability of reasonable care to eliminate risk, non-common usage, inappropriate location. Examples: blasting/explosives, storing large quantities of hazardous chemicals, keeping wild animals.

**Products Liability** (Restatement §402A): A commercial seller is strictly liable if a product is defective when sold and the defect causes harm to a user or consumer.[^20][^19]

**Three types of product defects:**

1. **Manufacturing defect**: The specific product deviated from its intended design. The product was made incorrectly. The standard: does this product conform to its own design specification?
2. **Design defect**: The entire product line is defective because the design itself is unreasonably dangerous. Two tests:
    - *Consumer expectations test*: The product failed to perform as safely as an ordinary consumer would expect
    - *Risk-utility test*: The foreseeable risks of harm outweigh the benefits of the design (common in CA — balancing test)
3. **Failure to warn (inadequate warning)**: Failure to provide sufficient instructions or warnings about known risks. Applies to non-obvious risks that render the product unreasonably dangerous when used without a warning.

**3D Printing Products Liability note for Javier**: When Typhon's Forge sells a 3D-printed functional accessory, it is in the chain of distribution as a **manufacturer and seller**. If a product you printed fails and causes harm — because of a material defect, a design flaw in your files, or inadequate warnings — strict liability applies. This is why: (1) material choice matters (food-safe vs. non-food-safe filament, heat-resistant materials), (2) your commission agreement should include a limitation of liability clause, (3) product liability insurance becomes relevant as revenue grows.

## 6. Civil Procedure — Federal Rules (FRCP 2025)[^21][^22][^23]

The Federal Rules of Civil Procedure govern the process of all civil cases in U.S. federal district courts. California state courts follow the California Code of Civil Procedure (CCP), which parallels FRCP in many but not all areas.

### Jurisdiction — Before Any Case Is Filed

**Subject Matter Jurisdiction (SMJ)**: Federal courts are courts of **limited jurisdiction** — they can only hear cases Congress has given them authority to hear.  Two primary bases:[^24]

**Federal Question Jurisdiction (28 U.S.C. §1331)**: The plaintiff's claim arises under federal law — the U.S. Constitution, federal statutes, or federal treaties. The federal issue must appear on the face of a well-pleaded complaint (not as a defense).

**Diversity Jurisdiction (28 U.S.C. §1332)**: Two requirements:

- Complete diversity: No plaintiff is a citizen of the same state as any defendant. Corporations are citizens of: their state of incorporation AND their principal place of business. Individuals are citizens of their domicile (where they intend to remain indefinitely).
- Amount in controversy exceeds \$75,000 (exclusive of interest and costs)

**Supplemental Jurisdiction**: If a federal court has SMJ over at least one claim, it may hear related state law claims arising from the same "case or controversy" (same transaction or occurrence).

**Personal Jurisdiction (PJ)**: The court's power over the defendant as a person.[^25][^24]

**Bases for personal jurisdiction:**

- **Domicile**: A state always has PJ over its own domiciliaries
- **Consent**: Defendant consents (by appearing without objecting, or by contract forum selection clause)
- **Presence**: Physical presence in the state when served (tag jurisdiction — still valid under *Burnham v. Superior Court*)
- **General Jurisdiction**: The defendant's contacts with the state are so "continuous and systematic" that they are essentially at home there. For corporations: state of incorporation or principal place of business.
- **Specific Jurisdiction**: For specific claims arising out of or related to defendant's contacts with the forum. Test from *International Shoe Co. v. Washington* (1945): defendant must have "minimum contacts" with the forum state such that maintenance of the suit does not offend "traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice."

**Specific jurisdiction refined by *Burger King*, *Asahi*, *Nicastro* (2011), *Bristol-Myers Squibb* (2017)**: The claim must arise directly from the contacts with the forum. Purposeful availment matters — did the defendant deliberately direct its activities toward the forum? A corporation cannot be sued in any state where some plaintiffs bought products if the specific plaintiffs' claims don't arise from that state's activities.

**Venue (28 U.S.C. §1391)**: Which judicial district (not just which state) the case is filed in. Proper in:

- Any district where any defendant resides (if all defendants reside in the same state)
- Any district where a substantial part of the events giving rise to the claim occurred
- For diversity cases: any district where any defendant is subject to PJ (if no other district qualifies)

**Removal (28 U.S.C. §1441)**: Defendant can remove a case from state to federal court if the federal court would have had original jurisdiction. Must remove within 30 days of receiving the complaint. Cannot remove a diversity case if any defendant is a citizen of the forum state. Plaintiff can move to remand if removal was improper.

### Pleading — Rules 7–12

**The Complaint (Rule 8)**: Must contain: (1) a short and plain statement of the court's jurisdiction, (2) a short and plain statement of the claim showing entitlement to relief, (3) a demand for judgment.[^22]

**Post-*Twombly/Iqbal* Plausibility Standard**: The complaint must allege facts that, taken as true, make the claim **plausible** — not merely possible. Conclusory allegations ("defendant was negligent") without supporting facts are insufficient. The court must be able to draw a reasonable inference that the defendant is liable. This significantly raised the bar from the old notice-pleading standard.

**Rule 12 Motions — Before/In Lieu of Answering:**


| Motion | Grounds |
| :-- | :-- |
| **12(b)(1)** | Lack of subject matter jurisdiction |
| **12(b)(2)** | Lack of personal jurisdiction |
| **12(b)(3)** | Improper venue |
| **12(b)(4)/(5)** | Insufficient process / service of process |
| **12(b)(6)** | Failure to state a claim upon which relief can be granted |
| **12(b)(7)** | Failure to join a necessary party |
| **12(c)** | Judgment on the pleadings (after pleadings close) |
| **12(f)** | Strike insufficient defenses or immaterial/scandalous matter |

**12(b)(6) Motion**: If the complaint is legally insufficient on its face — even accepting all factual allegations as true — the case should be dismissed. This is the primary tool for attacking inadequately pleaded complaints without discovery.

**The Answer (Rule 8)**: Defendant must admit, deny, or state lack of knowledge for each allegation. Must plead **all affirmative defenses** or they are waived (statute of limitations, statute of frauds, laches, contributory negligence, etc.).

### Discovery — Rules 26–37

Discovery is the pre-trial process of exchanging information relevant to the claims and defenses.  The scope is deliberately broad: parties may discover any non-privileged matter relevant to any party's claim or defense, proportional to the needs of the case.[^26][^22]

**Initial Disclosures (Rule 26(a)(1)) — Automatic, No Request Required:**

- Witnesses likely to have discoverable information
- Documents/ESI likely to be used to support claims or defenses
- Computation of each category of damages
- Insurance agreements

**Discovery Devices:**

**Interrogatories (Rule 33)**: Written questions answered in writing under oath, within 30 days. Limit of 25 interrogatories per party unless court permits more. Only parties (not non-party witnesses) can be interrogated.

**Depositions (Rules 27–32)**: Live questioning of witnesses under oath, transcribed (and often recorded). Any person — parties or non-parties — can be deposed. Duration: presumptive 7-hour limit. Number: presumptive 10 depositions per side without leave of court. Most valuable discovery tool because you get real-time answers without editing.

**Requests for Production (Rule 34)**: Demands for documents, ESI, and tangible items. Scope is broad — relevant to any claim or defense. Electronically stored information (ESI) obligations are extensive; parties must identify custodians and search methodologies.

**Requests for Admission (Rule 36)**: Requests to admit or deny specific facts or the genuineness of documents. Admitted facts are conclusively established for the case. Failure to respond within 30 days = deemed admitted.

**Expert Witnesses (Rule 26(a)(2))**: Parties must disclose expert witnesses and provide a written report (for retained experts) including: opinions, basis for opinions, data considered, exhibits, qualifications, prior testimony history, compensation. Experts can be deposed after report disclosure.

**Privilege Protection:**

- Attorney-client privilege: confidential communications between attorney and client for purpose of legal advice
- Work product doctrine (Rule 26(b)(3)): documents and tangible things prepared in anticipation of litigation by or for a party or its representative are protected from discovery (unless substantial need + undue hardship shown). Mental impressions, conclusions, opinions, legal theories of counsel are absolutely protected.

**Discovery Sanctions (Rule 37)**:

- Failure to comply with discovery → motion to compel → if granted, may award expenses
- Deliberate non-compliance or destruction of evidence (spoliation) → court may: exclude evidence, give adverse inference jury instruction, issue terminating sanctions (dismissal or default judgment)


### Dispositive Motions — Ending Cases Without Trial

**Summary Judgment (Rule 56)**: Any party may move for SJ on all or part of a claim, showing there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.[^24]

- Moving party's burden: Show absence of genuine dispute. Can meet burden by: (a) producing evidence negating an essential element of the non-movant's case, OR (b) showing the non-movant lacks evidence to support an essential element.
- Non-moving party's burden: Once movant meets their burden, non-movant must produce specific facts (not just allegations) showing a genuine dispute. Cannot rely on pleadings alone.
- Viewed in the light most favorable to the non-movant.

**Judgment as a Matter of Law (JMOL / Rule 50)**: During trial, if a party has been fully heard on an issue and no reasonable jury could find for that party, the court may grant JMOL on that issue. Can be renewed as a post-trial motion (RJNOV — Renewed Judgment Notwithstanding the Verdict).

### Appeals

**Finality requirement (28 U.S.C. §1291)**: Generally, only final judgments (ones that end the litigation on the merits) are immediately appealable. Most interlocutory orders (pretrial rulings) are not immediately appealable — you must wait for a final judgment and appeal all issues at once.

**Standards of Review:**

- Legal questions: **De novo** — court of appeals reviews the lower court's legal conclusions from scratch, no deference
- Factual findings: **Clear error** standard — court of appeals will not reverse unless the factual finding was clearly wrong (high deference to trial court)
- Discretionary rulings (discovery sanctions, evidentiary rulings): **Abuse of discretion** — reversed only if the lower court's decision was unreasonable
