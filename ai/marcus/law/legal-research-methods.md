# Legal Research Methods

## What Legal Research Actually Is

Legal research is the process of finding the law that governs a particular situation. "The law" consists of four sources in descending authority:

1. **Constitutional law** — federal and state constitutions (supreme authority)
2. **Statutory law** — legislation enacted by Congress or state legislatures (codified in the U.S. Code or California Codes)
3. **Regulatory law** — rules issued by agencies under statutory authority (Code of Federal Regulations, California Code of Regulations)
4. **Case law (common law)** — judicial decisions interpreting and applying the above

For most real-world legal questions, you'll be working primarily in statutory law + case law. Understanding how to find, read, and verify these sources is the skill.

---

## Step 1 — Find the Applicable Statute

### Federal Statutes
- **U.S. Code (USC)**: codifies all federal statutes by title and section
  - Free access: https://uscode.house.gov or https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text
  - Example: 26 U.S.C. § 1 = Title 26 (Internal Revenue), Section 1
- **Congress.gov**: find bills, track legislation, read full text: https://congress.gov

### California Statutes
- **California Codes**: the state's statutory law organized by subject area
  - Free at: https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov
  - Key codes for business/law: Business & Professions Code (BPC), Corporations Code (Corp. Code), Civil Code (CC), Code of Civil Procedure (CCP), Labor Code
  - Example: Cal. Corp. Code § 17701.01 = start of the LLC Act
- **Legislative Counsel of California**: publishes all codes in searchable format

### How to Read a Statutory Citation
```
26 U.S.C. § 501(c)(3)
↑     ↑       ↑
Title Code  Section + Subsection
```
```
Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 17200
↑              ↑           ↑
State         Code       Section
```

## Step 2 — Find Relevant Case Law

Case law interprets statutes and fills gaps. To find cases:

### Free Resources

**Google Scholar Legal** (https://scholar.google.com)
- Select "Case law" radio button
- Searches U.S. Supreme Court (back to 1791), federal circuit/district courts (1923+), state appellate courts
- Has a "How Cited" feature showing cases that cite your case (citator-lite)
- Not authoritative for citation purposes — use it to find cases, then verify elsewhere
- **Limitation**: does not reliably tell you if a case has been overruled

**CourtListener / Free Law Project** (https://www.courtlistener.com)
- Federal + some state courts
- Free, open-source, increasingly comprehensive
- Has a basic citator function ("cited by")

**Justia** (https://law.justia.com)
- Federal and state case law
- Free summaries and full text
- Better for finding old state court decisions than Google Scholar

**Cornell LII (Legal Information Institute)** (https://www.law.cornell.edu)
- U.S. Code, CFR, Supreme Court opinions
- Excellent for finding and reading statutes; less comprehensive for case search

**Fastcase** (https://www.fastcase.com)
- Some state bar associations provide free Fastcase access to members
- Law students often get access through school

### Paid Resources (Best for Serious Research)

**Westlaw** (Thomson Reuters)
- The gold standard. Comprehensive, authoritative, fast.
- **KeyCite**: Westlaw's citator — flags if a case is bad law (red flag = overruled or invalid, yellow flag = caution, questioned by other cases)
- Expensive ($100–$300+/month retail); law school students get free access through school subscriptions
- Student access through GCU library portal — check what's available

**LexisNexis**
- Near-equal to Westlaw in coverage
- **Shepard's Citations** ("Shepardizing"): the original citator system
  - Red stop sign = negative treatment (overruled, invalid)
  - Yellow triangle = caution (distinguished, criticized)
  - Green diamond = positive citing references
- Student access similar to Westlaw through law school subscriptions

**Bloomberg Law**
- Strong for transactional work, regulatory analysis, and dockets
- Own citator: BCite
- Good access through academic subscriptions

## Step 3 — Verify the Law is Still Good (Shepardizing / KeyCiting)

This is the most important step and the one beginners skip. A case that has been **overruled** by a higher court or later decision is no longer good law — if you rely on it, you're citing a legal dead letter.

### The Process
1. Find your case in Westlaw (KeyCite) or LexisNexis (Shepard's)
2. Look at the treatment flags:
   - **Red flag (Westlaw) / Red stop sign (Lexis)**: case is no longer good law on at least one issue — read why before citing
   - **Yellow flag (Westlaw) / Yellow triangle (Lexis)**: negative treatment; caution warranted
   - **Blue "C" (Westlaw)**: cited by other cases, no negative treatment — generally safe
3. Read the citing cases to understand what specific holding(s) have been affected
4. Note: a case can be partially overruled — the holding on Issue A may be bad law while the holding on Issue B remains valid

### Free alternative to Shepardizing
- Google Scholar's "How Cited" is a rough equivalent but is **not authoritative** and may miss overruling cases
- CourtListener has a basic citing function
- For academic work or important decisions: there is no free substitute for Westlaw/Lexis citators

## Step 4 — Read the Case

### Anatomy of a Case

```
Case name: McDonald v. City of Chicago, 561 U.S. 742 (2010)
           ↑ parties     ↑ volume ↑ reporter ↑ page ↑ year

Court: U.S. Supreme Court
Holding: The Second Amendment applies to state and local governments
         via the Due Process Clause of the 14th Amendment.

Structure of a case opinion:
1. Caption — case name, citation, court, date
2. Headnotes — numbered summaries of legal points (publisher-added, not law)
3. Facts — what happened
4. Procedural history — what courts have already decided
5. Issue(s) — what legal question(s) the court is resolving
6. Holding — the court's answer to each issue (THIS IS THE LAW)
7. Reasoning — why the court decided that way
8. Disposition — what happens next (affirmed, reversed, remanded)
9. Concurrences / Dissents — other judges' views (not binding, but persuasive)
```

### Briefing a Case (IRAC method)
For law school and self-study:
- **Issue**: What legal question does the case decide?
- **Rule**: What legal rule does the court apply?
- **Analysis**: How does the court apply the rule to the facts?
- **Conclusion**: What is the holding?

Adding:
- **Facts**: What facts were legally significant?
- **Procedural posture**: What happened in lower courts?

### Distinguishing Cases
A case is "distinguished" when a court says "the rule in that prior case applies to those facts, but not to the facts before us." This is how courts narrow or avoid precedent without overruling it. When you're researching, read how courts have distinguished a case you want to rely on — if it's been consistently distinguished, its practical authority may be weak.

## Research Workflow (TARP Method)

A structured research approach:

1. **T — Think** about what area of law governs (contracts, property, torts, constitutional, regulatory?)
2. **A — Annotated sources first** — start with secondary sources (treatises, law review articles, legal encyclopedias like Am Jur or Cal Jur) to understand the doctrine before diving into cases
3. **R — Read primary sources** — statutes first, then leading cases
4. **P — Primary authority** — verify everything is current, good law, correct jurisdiction

### Secondary Sources (Start Here for Unfamiliar Topics)
- **American Jurisprudence (Am. Jur.)**: national legal encyclopedia with topic overviews and case citations
- **California Jurisprudence (Cal. Jur.)**: California-specific encyclopedia
- **Law Review Articles**: Google Scholar → select "Articles" → search topic + "California" or "contracts"
- **Restatements**: authoritative summaries of common law rules (Restatement (Second) of Contracts; Restatement (Second) of Torts). Not law, but highly persuasive. Available free via LII and Westlaw.
- **Hornbooks**: law school textbooks. Casenote, Gilbert's, and Examples & Explanations series are excellent for self-study.

## Jurisdiction Matters — Always

A California court is not bound by a Texas court's decision. Know your jurisdiction:

### Federal court hierarchy (binding = lower courts must follow)
```
U.S. Supreme Court → binds all federal courts + state courts on federal law
    ↓
U.S. Circuit Courts of Appeals (13 circuits) → binds district courts within circuit
    ↓
U.S. District Courts → trial courts, no binding precedent
```
California is in the **9th Circuit** — 9th Circuit decisions bind California federal district courts.

### California state court hierarchy
```
California Supreme Court → binds all California courts
    ↓
California Courts of Appeal (6 districts) → binds trial courts within district
    ↓
Superior Courts → trial courts, no binding precedent
```

### Persuasive vs. Binding Authority
- **Binding**: same jurisdiction, higher court
- **Persuasive**: different jurisdiction, same level, dissents, secondary sources
- A well-reasoned opinion from the 2nd Circuit can be persuasive to a California federal court, but it's not binding

## For LSAT Prep — Logical Reasoning in Legal Research

The LSAT tests the same analytical skills used in legal research:
- Identifying the conclusion (= the holding)
- Identifying premises (= the facts and rules the court relies on)
- Identifying assumptions (= what the court assumes without stating)
- Evaluating argument structure (= IRAC)

Practice briefing cases from any area of law — contracts, torts, constitutional — as LSAT reading comprehension. The analytical structure is identical.
