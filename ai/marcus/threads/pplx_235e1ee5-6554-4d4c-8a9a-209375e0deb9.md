This MIT page is an introductory text explaining what calculus is, why we study it, and how a first calculus course is structured, with an emphasis on modeling change and empowering students to use computational tools.
math.mit

Core idea of calculus

Calculus is described as the study of how things change, giving a framework to model systems that change and to deduce quantitative predictions from those models.
math.mit

The “fundamental idea” is to study change via “instantaneous change,” meaning changes over tiny time intervals, because those are often much simpler to model than changes over long intervals.
math.mit

Why we study it

Calculus lets you build relatively simple quantitative models of changing systems (physical, economic, biological, etc.) and then deduce their consequences.
math.mit

By understanding how changing conditions affect a system, you can learn how to control it to behave in desired ways; this is framed as a source of “extraordinary power over the material world,” especially for engineers.
math.mit

The text argues that the development of calculus and its applications in physics and engineering were probably the key drivers of modern science’s development beyond Archimedes, fueling the industrial revolution and major advances over the last few centuries.
math.mit

What you study in a calculus course

For single-variable calculus (one input variable, like time):

You first need a framework for describing position, speed, and acceleration; motion along a path is described by assigning a single number to position at each time, and the collection of (time, position) pairs is a function.
math.mit

A typical course then covers:

How to find instantaneous change (the derivative) and rules for differentiation.

How to use derivatives to solve problems.

How to reconstruct a function from its derivative (integration).

Techniques for integrating various classes of functions.

Using integration for geometric problems such as areas and volumes.
math.mit

Additional standard topics include power series representations of functions and the study of convergence of infinite series.

Numbers, functions, and operations

The introductory chapter (before “real” calculus) revisits number systems: starting from natural numbers, then extending via subtraction, division, and square roots to negative numbers, rational numbers, and complex numbers; decimal expansions are used to present real numbers and connect to countability.
math.mit

The functions chapter begins with an abstract definition of a function as a set of argument–value pairs, then builds up “standard functions” from the identity function and the exponential function using operations such as addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, substitution, and inversion.
math.mit

The exponential function is characterized as the function that equals its own derivative and has value 1 at input 0, and it is noted to be closely related to the sine function from trigonometry.

Pedagogical twist: “empowerment”

The author notes that traditional calculus courses were designed mainly to build “mathematical sophistication” so students can read more advanced work, rather than to empower them to actually model and control systems themselves.
math.mit

This course explicitly tries to emphasize empowerment by:

Using computational tools like spreadsheets and applets to perform differentiation, integration, and solving ordinary differential equations with manageable effort.

Putting greater emphasis on building and analyzing models, especially differential-equation models of real systems.
