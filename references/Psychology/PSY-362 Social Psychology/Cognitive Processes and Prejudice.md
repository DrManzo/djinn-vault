# Cognitive Processes and Prejudice

## Summary
Prejudice can result from cognitive processes without malicious intent. Mechanisms like categorization, heuristics, the fundamental attribution error, and in-group bias operate automatically, leading to biased judgments even when intentions are neutral. Recognizing these unconscious processes is essential for mitigating their effects.

## Key Points
- Cognitive processes such as categorization and reliance on heuristics produce prejudice without deliberate malice
- The fundamental attribution error leads people to overemphasize dispositional factors and underestimate situational influences
- Social categorization and in-group-out-group dynamics influence perceptions below conscious awareness
- People favor those in their in-group and harbor biases against out-group members without conscious intent
- Confirmation bias entrenches prejudices by favoring information that confirms existing beliefs
- Social learning can adopt biases found in the surrounding environment automatically
- Similar-to-me bias in hiring leads people to prefer candidates who think and behave like themselves
- Regional bias examples show groups believing others feel superior, reinforcing stereotypes without understanding
- Alma mater bias demonstrates how in-group favoritism feels logical yet excludes others unfairly
- Cognitive shortcuts are especially active under cognitive load, distraction, or limited mental resources
- Awareness and critical thinking are necessary to challenge automatic biases and ensure fairer judgments
- Prejudice from cognitive processes highlights that intent is not always a factor in discriminatory outcomes
- Thoughtful examination of automatic beliefs and behaviors is needed before they shape outcomes

## Details
An example of prejudice without malice occurs when a hiring manager prefers applicants from their own alma mater, believing them to be more compatible simply due to shared experience, not out of animosity. Another example involves assuming an older coworker would not be tech-savvy, only to learn they were more skilled. These experiences show that even small, unintentional judgments can lead to exclusion. The brain naturally sorts people into groups and reacts with bias before conscious awareness. When someone does not fit the mold of what is familiar, the mind may see them as an outsider simply because they challenge expectations. This is not about intentional bias but about how the brain copes with uncertainty by turning to the safety of what is known. Without realizing it, people can push those who are different to the margins. It often takes reflection or witnessing someone surpass stereotypes to recognize how limiting these assumptions can be.
