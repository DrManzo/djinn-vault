# 12 Angry Men Analysis

## Summary
The film 12 Angry Men vividly illustrates core social psychology concepts including minority influence, conformity, groupthink, group polarization, stereotyping, prejudice, and aggression escalation. Juror 8's lone not-guilty vote demonstrates how a consistent minority can gradually persuade the majority through logical reasoning and persistent questioning of evidence.

## Key Points
- Minority influence operates through Juror 8's consistent, calm dissent against eleven guilty votes
- Conformity appears as jurors initially vote guilty to avoid being singled out or rejected
- Normative social influence drives timid jurors to align with the group rather than risk being outcasts
- Informational social influence occurs as logical arguments gradually shift votes through systematic evidence evaluation
- Stereotypes and prejudice influence decisions based on the defendant's ethnicity and language skills
- Linguistic prejudice appears when jurors mock the defendant's imperfect English
- Attribution bias manifests in Juror 3 projecting personal resentment about his son onto the defendant
- Zillman's three stages of escalation appear through heat-induced irritation, intensifying anger, and explosive aggression
- Situational causes of aggression include oppressive heat, confinement, and time pressure
- Biological causes include Juror 3's unresolved trauma and emotional dysregulation
- Cultural causes include generational frustration and class consciousness
- Groupthink initially drives the 11-1 guilty consensus without critical evidence evaluation
- Group polarization occurs as attitudes intensify in both directions during deliberation
- The film shows how reasoned dissent can overcome collective bias through patience and evidence

## Details
The jury's early decision-making epitomizes groupthink, with fear of social rejection driving conformity. Several jurors initially vote guilty merely to avoid conflict. As discussions continue, group polarization occurs with attitudes intensifying as debate progresses. Those favoring not guilty grow more confident as evidence weakens the prosecution's claims. Key turning points include the reconsideration of witness testimony, the knife demonstration, and the exposure of emotional bias in Juror 3. The man with deep-seated resentment toward his son tries to take control, aggressively shaming others. His ultimate explosion comes when he shouts the exact phrase he insisted proved the defendant's guilt, then destroys a picture of himself and his son. The film explores the collective unconscious and how outside voices influence inner judgment. It takes conviction to persist in what feels like a losing battle, even when you are right. By the end, the group dynamic evolves from conformity to critical dialogue, illustrating how reasoned dissent overcomes collective bias.
