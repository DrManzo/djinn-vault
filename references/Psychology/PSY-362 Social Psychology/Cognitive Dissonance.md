# Cognitive Dissonance

## Summary
Cognitive dissonance occurs when individuals experience psychological discomfort from holding conflicting beliefs, attitudes, or behaviors. People are motivated to reduce this discomfort through attitude change, rationalization, or seeking confirming information. The concept explains why individuals justify unethical actions and resist changing their views even when presented with contradictory evidence.

## Key Points
- Cognitive dissonance creates psychological discomfort from holding conflicting cognitions
- People are motivated to reduce dissonance through attitude change or rationalization
- Justifying unethical actions reduces dissonance by aligning behavior with self-concept
- The Stanford Prison Experiment showed participants justifying increasingly cruel behavior
- Selective exposure leads people to seek information confirming existing beliefs
- Post-decision dissonance occurs after making difficult choices between alternatives
- Effort justification causes people to value outcomes more when they worked hard to achieve them
- Insufficient justification occurs when external rewards are too small to explain behavior
- Dual attitudes allow people to hold both implicit and explicit attitudes simultaneously
- Group members experiencing dissonance may conform more strongly to reduce discomfort
- The agitated juror in 12 Angry Men experienced dissonance when baited into proving his own hypocrisy
- Changing behavior is often harder than changing attitudes to resolve dissonance
- Self-affirmation can reduce the need to defensively justify inconsistent beliefs

## Details
Cognitive dissonance played a significant role in the Stanford Prison Experiment as guards justified their increasingly abusive actions. Rather than acknowledging their behavior was wrong, guards rationalized their actions as necessary for maintaining order. Similarly, in 12 Angry Men, the juror with deep-seated resentment toward his son experienced dissonance when his own aggressive outburst mirrored the exact behavior he claimed proved the defendant's guilt. When baited into shouting threats, he realized his own hypocrisy, creating intense psychological discomfort. The resolution came through emotional breakdown rather than rational acceptance. In persuasion contexts, insufficient justification explains why small rewards can be more effective than large ones at changing attitudes. When external justification is minimal, people must internally justify their behavior by actually changing their attitudes. This principle underlies many effective persuasion techniques and explains why forced compliance can lead to genuine attitude change.
