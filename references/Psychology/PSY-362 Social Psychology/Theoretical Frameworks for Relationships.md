# Theoretical Frameworks for Relationships

## Summary
Multiple theoretical frameworks explain relationship formation and maintenance, including attachment theory, the investment model, Sternberg's triangular theory, evolutionary perspectives, and interdependence theory. Each framework offers unique insights into why people connect, what sustains commitment, and how relationships evolve over time.

## Key Points
- Attachment theory links early caregiver bonds to adult relationship patterns and conflict styles
- Secure attachment enables open communication and constructive conflict resolution
- Anxious attachment leads to frequent reassurance-seeking and fear of abandonment
- Avoidant attachment causes withdrawal during emotional intimacy and discomfort with closeness
- The investment model explains commitment through satisfaction, alternatives, and accumulated investments
- Couples persist through difficulties because shared history and resources make separation costly
- Sternberg's triangular theory identifies intimacy, passion, and commitment as components of love
- Companionate love involves intimacy and commitment remaining even when passion diminishes
- Fatuous love combines passion and commitment without the foundation of intimacy
- Evolutionary perspectives explain mate preferences through survival and reproductive optimization
- Individualistic cultures emphasize personal choice and independence within relationships
- Collectivist cultures value family opinions, group harmony, and social obligations
- Gender roles influence relationship dynamics, with traditional roles creating power imbalances
- Couples who align on gender role expectations report higher relationship satisfaction regardless of type

## Details
Attachment theory and the investment model complement each other by explaining both emotional foundations and practical commitment decisions. Attachment styles shape how individuals approach emotional intimacy and conflict resolution, while investment considerations influence long-term commitment decisions. Sternberg's theory adds nuance by identifying different love types based on component combinations. In individualistic cultures, people prioritize personal happiness and desires when forming or ending relationships. In collectivist cultures, relationships are influenced by family opinions and group expectations, with more traditional gender roles often enforced. Gender roles continue to influence modern relationships though their impact is evolving. While most express belief in gender equality, many still maintain some traditional expectations in practice. The most successful relationships combine emotional security with practical commitment. Neither passionate feelings nor calculated investments alone sustain long-term partnerships. Both elements contribute to relationship resilience and growth.
