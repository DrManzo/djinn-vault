# Multisensory Processing Abnormalities

## Summary
Abnormalities in multisensory processing reveal fundamental mechanisms of how the brain integrates information across sensory channels. Studying deficits in conditions like autism spectrum disorder and schizophrenia illuminates principles of normal sensory convergence, cross-modal plasticity, and the brain's remarkable capacity to reorganize when sensory input changes.

## Key Points
- The temporal binding window (TBW) defines the time period over which the brain treats signals from different senses as belonging to the same event; individuals with schizophrenia exhibit consistently widened TBWs
- In autism, widened TBWs arise from atypical prior expectations about sensory co-occurrence, while in schizophrenia they reflect both altered priors and degraded sensory representations
- Developmental diaschisis demonstrates how dysfunction in one brain area cascades to affect distant, anatomically connected regions during development
- The principle of inverse effectiveness shows that weak sensory stimuli benefit more from multisensory enhancement than strong stimuli; this mechanism is disrupted in clinical populations
- Cross-modal plasticity can dramatically reorganize cortical maps—following early blindness, the "visual" cortex actively processes tactile, auditory, and even olfactory information
- Primary sensory cortices are already multisensory, receiving cross-modal inputs that are actively gated by local inhibitory circuit dynamics
- Sensory modalities function as partially specialized computational streams within highly interconnected networks rather than isolated systems

## Details
The superior colliculus has been instrumental in revealing multisensory integration principles. Its multisensory neurons require spatially and temporally congruent cross-modal inputs for enhancement, and this capability depends critically on cortical inputs from the anterior ectosylvian sulcus.

Integration occurs at multiple hierarchical levels—not confined to heteromodal association cortices but operating in parallel across cortical and subcortical structures. The thalamus, traditionally viewed as a modality-specific relay, participates directly in multisensory integration.

The brain employs two fundamental principles: functional specialization (localization of specific computations) and functional integration (coordination among specialized areas through effective connectivity). These are complementary, not competing, organizing principles.
