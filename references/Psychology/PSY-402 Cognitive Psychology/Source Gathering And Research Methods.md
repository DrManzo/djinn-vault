# Source Gathering And Research Methods

## Summary
Effective research in cognitive psychology requires systematic approaches to locating, evaluating, and synthesizing scholarly sources. Understanding research methodologies, source evaluation criteria, and evidence-based practices ensures that academic work is grounded in credible, peer-reviewed literature and contributes meaningfully to the field.

## Key Points
- Peer-reviewed journal articles represent the gold standard for academic research, having undergone rigorous evaluation by subject-matter experts before publication
- Primary sources (original research studies) provide direct evidence, while secondary sources (reviews, meta-analyses) synthesize existing findings and identify patterns across studies
- Effective literature searches use Boolean operators (AND, OR, NOT), controlled vocabulary (MeSH terms, PsycINFO thesaurus), and citation chaining (forward and backward) to locate relevant sources
- Source evaluation requires assessing credibility (author expertise, institutional affiliation), currency (publication date, relevance to current knowledge), accuracy (methodological rigor, statistical validity), and bias (funding sources, conflicts of interest)
- Meta-analyses and systematic reviews provide the highest level of evidence by quantitatively synthesizing results across multiple studies, reducing the influence of individual study limitations
- Research methodologies in cognitive psychology include experimental designs (establishing causation), correlational studies (identifying relationships), case studies (in-depth analysis of individual cases), and neuroimaging techniques (fMRI, EEG, PET)

## Details
The research process in cognitive psychology follows a systematic progression: identifying a research question, conducting a comprehensive literature review, selecting appropriate methodology, collecting and analyzing data, and drawing evidence-based conclusions. Each step requires careful attention to validity (are we measuring what we intend to measure?) and reliability (are our measurements consistent?).

For students and practitioners, developing research literacy means understanding not just what studies find, but how they find it. This includes recognizing common methodological limitations (small sample sizes, WEIRD populations, publication bias), interpreting effect sizes alongside p-values, and distinguishing between statistical significance and practical importance. These skills are essential for both consuming research critically and conducting rigorous studies.
