# Weighted Mean And Central Tendency

## Summary
Central tendency identifies a single value representative of an entire distribution, with three primary measures: mean, median, and mode. The weighted mean should be used when data points vary in importance, frequency, or representativeness, providing superior population representation compared to the arithmetic mean by proportionally accounting for subgroup disparities.

## Key Points
- The **mean** (arithmetic average) is calculated by summing all values and dividing by the number of observations; best for normal distributions without extreme outliers
- The **median** is the middle value when observations are ordered; preferred for skewed distributions or when extreme scores exist
- The **mode** is the most frequently occurring value; used for nominal scale data or identifying the most common category
- The most common mistake is using the mean for skewed distributions — the mean gets pulled toward extreme observations, misrepresenting the data's center
- The weighted mean assigns differential importance to values based on their contribution to the population being studied
- Weighted mean formula: x̄w = Σ(wi × xi) / Σwi, where wi is the weight for each value xi
- Weighted means control for sampling biases and demographic imbalances by assigning appropriate weights to subgroups
- In a multi-campus university example, arithmetic mean gave GPA of 3.5 while weighted mean gave 3.45 — the weighted value accurately reflected the larger campus's influence
- In normal distributions, mean, median, and mode are identical; in skewed distributions, the median lies between the mean and mode
- The mean is generally the best measure because it uses every value and most resists fluctuation between samples
- The median is preferred when extreme scores exist, values are undetermined, or data uses ordinal scales
- Weighted means make variables more isolated and manageable, allowing researchers to adjust each subgroup's influence on aggregate measures

## Details

### When to Use Each Measure
| Measure | Best For | Scale Type |
|---------|----------|------------|
| Mean | Normal distributions, no outliers | Interval/Ratio |
| Median | Skewed distributions, outliers present | Ordinal/Interval/Ratio |
| Mode | Categorical data, most common value | Nominal |
| Weighted Mean | Subgroups of different sizes/importance | Any (with weights) |

### Multimodal Distributions
When a distribution has two distinct modes, reporting both modes may provide a more accurate reflection of the dataset than using the median. For example, test scores from two different schools may each have their own mode, and the median could obscure the presence of these two distinct groups.
