# Internal and External Validity

## Summary
This note examines the tension between internal and external validity in experimental research, explaining why experimenters typically prioritize internal validity when both cannot be achieved. It explores the theory-testing mode of research and how researchers balance methodological rigor with real-world applicability.

## Key Points
- Internal validity is the foundation upon which any meaningful causal inference rests
- Without internal validity, researchers cannot confidently determine what caused the outcome, making results potentially misleading
- The rigorous controls necessary for high internal validity (randomization, blinding, standardized procedures) often create artificial conditions that limit generalizability
- Internal validity is a necessary condition of external validity — without first establishing that a causal relationship exists, claims about generalizability are speculative
- Controlled settings serve as laboratories for clarity, not replicas of reality
- Generalizing flawed findings does more harm than good
- Researchers operating in theory-testing mode often forego random sampling to prioritize internal validity
- Theory-testing studies focus on isolating specific variables under controlled conditions to validate or refine theoretical models
- Convenience sampling or laboratory settings eliminate confounding factors, ensuring observed effects stem from manipulated variables
- Researchers in theory-testing mode emphasize methodological rigor, precise measurement tools, and replication potential over broad population representation
- Construct validity ensures measurements accurately reflect the studied concepts, while controlled conditions reduce noise from extraneous variables
- A phased approach is common: begin with tightly controlled experiments to confirm causal mechanisms, then move toward diverse and representative samples to enhance generalizability

## Details
The prioritization of internal validity stems from a fundamental principle: causality cannot be inferred without isolating the variable of interest. While external validity asks whether findings matter to the world, internal validity first demands whether findings matter within the study itself.

Researchers, like cartographers, must first sketch accurate maps of small, controlled territories before claiming to navigate the vastness of the world. Science advances not by chasing breadth prematurely, but by deepening its grasp of what is true in the microcosm before extrapolating to the macro.

The trade-off between internal and external validity is not a flaw in research design but a strategic choice. The decision on which to prioritize depends on the goals of the research. For studies aiming to prove cause and effect, internal validity comes first. However, considering external validity remains important for making research findings meaningful beyond the laboratory.
