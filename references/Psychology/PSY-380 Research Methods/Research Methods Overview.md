# Research Methods Overview

## Summary
This note covers practical applications of research methods including science journalism evaluation, polling methodology, and sampling strategies. It examines how organizations like Pew Research obtain representative samples, the challenges of polling cell-phone-only households, and how to critically evaluate media coverage of psychological research.

## Key Points
- Scientific polling relies on sampling methods designed to represent the population as a whole, not surveying every individual
- Random-digit dialing (RDD) allows pollsters to randomly select phone numbers across geographic areas to create representative samples
- A well-designed sample can yield accurate insights into public opinion without contacting everyone
- Non-response bias occurs when certain groups systematically avoid participation, affecting accuracy
- Social desirability bias leads respondents to provide answers they believe are socially acceptable rather than their true opinions
- Dual-frame sampling includes both landline and cell phone numbers through RDD
- Federal regulations prohibit auto-dialing cell phones, requiring manual dialing and increasing costs
- Address-based sampling (ABS) uses postal databases to mail surveys regardless of phone type
- Statistical weighting aligns findings with national demographic benchmarks
- Pew Research intentionally over-samples mobile users (65% of interviews) to counterbalance historical landline biases
- When evaluating science journalism, compare the media article's claims against the empirical study's actual findings
- Media articles often use causal language when the empirical study only identifies correlations
- Media coverage tends to oversimplify variables, omit moderating factors, and overgeneralize sample findings
- The National Health Interview Survey (NHIS) provides foundational data on household communication trends
- Anecdotal evidence in media coverage may lead readers to overgeneralize rare or extreme cases as typical outcomes
- Correlation does not imply causation; other factors could contribute to observed outcomes

## Details
The woman who claimed polling was invalid because she had not been called demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of sampling. Scientific polling does not require contacting every individual — a properly designed random sample can accurately represent the population. The statistical principles underlying random sampling make it unnecessary to survey everyone.

When critiquing media coverage of psychological research, evaluate whether the media article accurately represents the study's variables, claims, results, and sample. Look for discrepancies between causal claims in the media and correlational findings in the research, and note whether moderating factors or sample limitations are acknowledged.
