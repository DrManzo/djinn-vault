II. **Forum Selection: Selecting the Proper Forum:**

Forum selection determines where a case may properly be filed.

1.  **Personal Jurisdiction (PJ)**\
    The court must have power over the defendant or property.

    - Specific personal jurisdiction (minimum contacts analysis)

    - General personal jurisdiction ("at home" standard)

    - Transient jurisdiction (service in the state)

2.  **Subject Matter Jurisdiction (SMJ)**\
    The court must have authority to hear the type of case.

> a\. **Diversity Jurisdiction**\
> Federal courts may hear cases between citizens of different states
> where the amount in controversy exceeds \$75,000.
>
> b\. **Federal Question Jurisdiction**\
> Federal courts may hear cases arising under the Constitution, federal
> laws, or treaties.

3.  **Supplemental Jurisdiction**\
    Federal courts may hear additional related claims that arise from
    the same transaction or occurrence.

4.  **Removal and Remand**\
    Defendants may remove cases from state court to federal court if
    jurisdiction exists; improper removal may result in remand.

5.  **Venue**\
    Venue determines the proper geographic federal district court.

> a\. **Residential Venue**\
> Based on where defendants reside (if all reside in the same state).
>
> b\. **Transactional Venue**\
> Based on where a substantial part of the events or property is
> located.
>
> c\. **Fallback Venue Rule**\
> Applies when no other venue is proper.
>
> d\. **Transfer of Venue**\
> Cases may be transferred between federal courts for convenience or if
> venue is improper.
>
> e\. **Forum Selection Clauses**\
> Parties may contractually agree to a specific forum.
>
> f\. **Forum Non Conveniens**\
> Courts may dismiss cases when another judicial system is more
> appropriate.

**Civil Procedure Overview**

**1. Introduction to Civil Procedure**

Civil procedure provides the ground rules for any civil, meaning
noncriminal, case in the court system. It covers everything from how to
file a lawsuit, through pretrial procedures, and what motions you can
bring, as well as how and when you can appeal.

Other subjects provide the "what" for lawsuits, meaning what substantive
right you are suing someone over, what elements must be proved, and what
remedies are available.

Civil procedure provides the "how," meaning how you go about getting the
case into court and how the court decides who wins.

Civil procedure is far removed from the average person's everyday
experiences. People commonly enter into contracts, from buying a cup of
coffee to buying a car, but unless someone has been a party to a lawsuit
and was intricately involved in the process, they probably have not had
real-life experience with civil procedure.

**2. Governing Rules**

Each state has its own civil procedure code.

The focus here is on the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure (FRCP).

The Federal Rules are the only rules tested on multi-state exams, and
many states have based their civil procedure rules on the FRCP.

**3. Three Broad Areas of Civil Procedure**

Civil procedure can be broken down into three broad areas:

1.  Forum selection

2.  The litigation process

3.  Appeals and the effect of a judgment

These areas may overlap at times.

**4. Forum Selection**

Forum selection is about deciding where to file a case.

This decision depends on several rules.

> **A. Personal Jurisdiction**
>
> The first requirement is that the forum must have power over the
> defendant or the property involved in the case.
>
> For **people**, this is called **personal jurisdiction**, also known
> as in personam jurisdiction.
>
> For **property**, this is called in **rem jurisdiction,** which means
> jurisdiction over a thing.
>
> The specific rules governing personal jurisdiction and in rem
> jurisdiction are addressed separately.
>
> **B. Subject Matter Jurisdiction**
>
> Subject matter jurisdiction refers to whether a court has the power to
> hear the type of case the plaintiff wants to bring.
>
> Every state has at least one court of general subject matter
> jurisdiction that can hear any claim arising under state or federal
> law, unless the federal law is limited to federal courts.
>
> Federal courts, however, are courts of limited subject matter
> jurisdiction and can only hear certain types of cases.
>
> The two primary types of cases federal courts can hear are diversity
> cases and federal question cases.
>
> **1. Diversity Jurisdiction**
>
> In civil procedure, diversity refers to whether the plaintiff and
> defendant are from different states.
>
> This concept originated from concerns at the time the Constitution was
> written that a person from one state might not receive fair treatment
> in another state's courts.
>
> Under certain conditions, diversity jurisdiction allows a person from
> one state to sue someone from another state in federal court.
>
> The specific conditions for diversity jurisdiction are addressed
> separately.
>
> **2. Federal Question Jurisdiction**
>
> Federal district courts have the power to hear all civil cases arising
> under federal law.
>
> A case arises under federal law if the plaintiff is alleging a right
> or interest that is substantially founded on federal law, including
> the Constitution, laws, or treaties of the United States.
>
> **C. Supplemental Jurisdiction**
>
> There are rules that allow federal courts to hear closely related
> state law claims in addition to claims that qualify for federal
> jurisdiction.
>
> This is done in the interest of judicial economy and is known as
> supplemental jurisdiction.
>
> **D. Venue**
>
> Venue refers to the geographic location where a case may be brought.
>
> In federal cases, venue is governed by statute.
>
> The statutes may allow more than one possible location, and there are
> also rules that allow a case to be transferred to a more convenient
> forum.

**5. The Litigation Process**

The litigation process refers to the rules that control how a case
proceeds once it is filed.

> **A. Beginning a Case**
>
> In federal court, a case begins when the plaintiff drafts a complaint.
>
> The complaint informs the defendant of the plaintiff's claims, and
> there are rules governing what must be included and how much detail is
> required.
>
> The plaintiff then files the complaint in a court that is proper under
> the forum selection rules.
>
> **B. Service of Process**
>
> After filing the complaint, the plaintiff must ensure that the
> defendant is notified of the case.
>
> This is done by serving the defendant with a copy of the complaint.
>
> The plaintiff must properly serve the defendant according to the
> rules, which include requirements about timing and methods.
>
> Service methods can include handing the papers directly to the
> defendant or, in some cases, providing notice through publication.
>
> **C. Defendant's Initial Response**
>
> After being served, the defendant does not immediately go to trial.
>
> Instead, the defendant may challenge the case by filing an answer or
> various motions.
>
> These motions may challenge issues such as:

- Subject matter jurisdiction

- Venue

- Sufficiency of service

- Sufficiency of the complaint

> The goal of these motions is often to have the case dismissed or
> transferred before trial.
>
> **D. Counterclaims**
>
> The defendant may also file a counterclaim against the plaintiff.
>
> Counterclaims are not based on vindictiveness but are filed because
> the defendant believes they have been injured as well.
>
> There are rules requiring that claims have factual and legal support.
>
> **E. Discovery**
>
> If the case proceeds, the parties must disclose evidence to each
> other.
>
> This process is known as discovery.
>
> Although it may seem unusual for parties to reveal their evidence,
> discovery serves several purposes:

- It helps parties evaluate the strength of their case

- It may lead to settlement

- It can narrow the issues for trial

> The parties may also agree on certain facts through stipulations,
> which can simplify the trial.
>
> **F. Trial**
>
> If the case continues, it proceeds to trial.
>
> If the plaintiff has a right to a jury trial and requests one, the
> case will be heard by a jury.
>
> **1. Jury Selection (Voir Dire)**
>
> Jurors are selected through a process called voir dire.
>
> During voir dire, lawyers question potential jurors to ensure they can
> be fair.
>
> There are rules governing jury selection, including:

- When a jury trial is allowed

- The number of jurors

- Valid reasons for excluding jurors

- The vote required for a verdict

> **2. Rules of Evidence**
>
> Rules of evidence apply during trial, although they are considered a
> separate subject from civil procedure.
>
> **G. Judgment**
>
> After trial, either the jury renders a verdict or the judge issues a
> judgment.

**6. Appeals**

The losing party has the right to appeal the decision.

There are rules governing the timing and method for filing an appeal.

**7. Effect of Judgment (Preclusion)**

> After the appeals process is complete, the case may still have ongoing
> legal effects.
>
> If a party attempts to bring another lawsuit involving the same
> transaction or similar issues, the court may apply preclusion
> doctrines.
>
> **A. Claim Preclusion (Res Judicata)**
>
> Claim preclusion prevents a party from relitigating the same claim
> after a final judgment.
>
> **B. Issue Preclusion (Collateral Estoppel)**
>
> Issue preclusion prevents a party from relitigating an issue that was
> already decided in a previous case.

**8. Final Perspective**

Civil procedure may not seem exciting, but it functions like a set of
rules for a game.

Litigation is similar to a game like Monopoly, where players must follow
the rules in order to participate.

Even though learning the rules may not be enjoyable, they are necessary
to understand how the system works.

**Selecting the Proper Forum:\
Subject Matter Jurisdiction\**

**1. Introduction and Case Setup**

In thinking about civil procedure, it is helpful to think about a case
from beginning to end. So let's begin at the beginning.

Let's say that Samantha wants Amber to represent her in a civil matter.
At the client intake interview, Samantha explains that she was stopped
at a red light and then was rear-ended by someone named Julia.

Samantha says she had just driven out of Wisconsin, where she had been
shopping, and into Illinois, where she lives with her parents. According
to their statement on the police report, Julia lives in Wisconsin.

Samantha says she was hit pretty hard. Her car was totaled, and it
appears she sustained a concussion when her airbags deployed. She also
says her nose was broken and that she is still suffering from bad
headaches.

**2. Initial Legal Question: What Should Amber Do?**

So what does Amber do with all this information?

Well, the first thing Amber has to consider is where Samantha can file
suit. To figure this out, Amber has to consider the rules about subject
matter jurisdiction, venue, and personal jurisdiction.

All three are pieces of the puzzle when it comes to selecting the right
court.

**3. Subject Matter Jurisdiction**

**A. State Courts vs. Federal Courts**

As an initial matter, the choice is broadly between federal court and
state court.

The United States has state and federal court systems operating side by
side, and in fact, a state may have state courts and federal courts
operating within literal blocks of each other.

Generally, the state courts are courts of general subject matter
jurisdiction, meaning that state courts can hear nearly all cases. That
is, some state courts somewhere generally can hear a case regardless of
the type of case.

State court is always an option, and state courts can even hear most
cases involving federal law. There are a few exceptions for exclusively
federal issues like bankruptcy, but car accident cases do not fall into
that category.

So some Illinois state court will have subject matter jurisdiction over
Samantha's case.

**B. Federal Courts and Their Limitations**

Amber might be able to file Samantha's case in federal court in lieu of
state court, but maybe not, because unlike state courts, federal courts
are courts of limited jurisdiction.

The only cases they can hear are the cases that the Constitution and
federal statutes say they can hear.

The limited bases for federal subject matter jurisdiction are primarily
federal question jurisdiction and diversity of citizenship jurisdiction.

**C. Federal Question Jurisdiction**

Federal question jurisdiction requires that the case arise under a
federal statute, a treaty, or the Constitution. The plaintiff's claim
simply has to arise under federal law.

In Samantha's case, her claim does not arise under federal law. Car
accidents are tort claims, and they are controlled by state tort law.

Additionally, the federal issue must be one that would come up in the
plaintiff's well-pleaded complaint. The defendant's answer or something
that the plaintiff puts into their complaint anticipating a federal
defense does not make the case arise under federal law.

For example, if a political figure brings a libel action under state law
against a journalist, freedom of speech may come up as a defense, but
that is not part of the plaintiff's complaint, so it is not enough to
make the case arise under federal law.

**D. Diversity of Citizenship Jurisdiction**

Diversity of citizenship jurisdiction gives federal courts subject
matter jurisdiction over some claims based on state law, assuming that
two conditions are met.

First, the claim must exceed \$75,000. Second, there must be complete
diversity, which means that no plaintiff shares state citizenship with
any defendant.

Even if these conditions are met, the plaintiff still could file the
case in state court. It is the plaintiff's choice initially.

**1. Citizenship and Domicile**

An individual is a citizen of the state of which they are domiciled, and
they keep this domicile until they change it. This is determined as of
the moment when the suit is filed.

Samantha lives in Illinois with her parents, so she is likely domiciled
in Illinois. Julia stated on the police report that she lives in
Wisconsin, so we can assume she is domiciled in Wisconsin.

Because Samantha is the only plaintiff and she is domiciled in Illinois,
and Julia is the only defendant and she is domiciled in Wisconsin, there
is complete diversity.

**2. Example Breaking Diversity**

If a third driver named Tia is added to the case and Tia lives in
Illinois like Samantha, then Samantha could not sue Julia and Tia in
federal court because there would not be complete diversity.

The plaintiff, Samantha, would share the same domicile, Illinois, as one
of the defendants, Tia.

**3. Changing Domicile**

A person keeps their domicile until they change it. To change domicile,
a person must be physically present in a new state and must have the
intent to remain there permanently.

Both physical presence and intent are required.

For example, a person like Jed who intends to move to California does
not change domicile until he physically arrives there. Intent alone is
not enough.

Similarly, a student who goes to school out of state does not change
domicile if they intend to return home after school.

**4. Amount in Controversy**

The claim must exceed \$75,000, and this must be alleged in good faith.

Samantha spent time in the hospital, continues to suffer from headaches,
and had her car totaled. These damages can reasonably exceed \$75,000.

We can include all potential damages, including pain and suffering, and
we do not consider potential defenses or counterclaims when determining
the amount.

**5. Defenses and Counterclaims Do Not Matter**

If Julia claims that Samantha's brake lights were out, that would reduce
Samantha's recovery, but it does not affect the amount in controversy
because it is a defense.

If Julia files a counterclaim for damage to her own car, that also does
not affect the amount in controversy because it is a counterclaim.

**6. Challenging the Amount**

Julia can challenge Samantha's allegation that damages exceed \$75,000,
but she would have to show that Samantha cannot recover more than
\$75,000 to a legal certainty, which is a difficult burden.

**7. If the Final Award Is Less**

If Samantha is awarded less than \$75,000 at trial, this does not
destroy jurisdiction. The amount pleaded in good faith in the complaint
is sufficient.

**Conclusion on Diversity Jurisdiction**

The requirements for diversity jurisdiction appear to be met, so the
case could be filed in federal court or in state court, and it is up to
Samantha to decide.

**4. Supplemental Jurisdiction**

Supplemental jurisdiction is an additional grant of subject matter
jurisdiction.

It applies when there is a claim already in federal court under
diversity or federal question jurisdiction, and there is another related
claim that does not independently qualify for federal jurisdiction.

The related claim must arise from a "common nucleus of operative fact,"
which generally means it arises from the same transaction or occurrence.

For example, if Julia's car sustained \$16,000 in damage and Julia wants
to file a counterclaim, that claim would not meet the \$75,000
requirement on its own, but it could be heard in federal court under
supplemental jurisdiction because it arises from the same accident.

However, original plaintiffs in diversity cases generally cannot use
supplemental jurisdiction.

**5. Removal**

If Samantha files in Illinois state court and Julia does not want to be
in state court, Julia can attempt to remove the case to federal court.

Only the defendant can remove a case, so Samantha cannot remove.

If there is more than one defendant, all defendants who have been served
must agree to the removal.

There must also be federal subject matter jurisdiction for removal to be
proper.

**Improper Removal and Remand**

If a case is not properly removed, the plaintiff can move to have the
case remanded back to state court.

There are timing rules and restrictions related to removal and remand,
but those are not covered here.

**6. What Comes Next: Venue**

Up to this point, the discussion has only addressed whether a case
belongs in federal or state court. It has not addressed which specific
court to file in, such as which federal district court or which state
county court. That issue is called venue. Subject matter jurisdiction
determines whether a case can be heard in federal court, and venue
determines which specific court is the proper location for the case.

**Q &A**

1.  You represent a consumer who would like to bring an action based on
    federal lending statutes. The consumer claims damages in excess of
    \$100,000 based on the statute. The consumer is a citizen of
    California, whereas the lender is a citizen solely of Delaware.  \
    \
    Which of the following best sums up subject matter jurisdiction
    here? 

A. the federal court has exclusive jurisdiction because the case is
based on federal statue.

b\. a federal court can hear the case under either federal question or
diversity jurisdiction.

C. SMJ must be based on diversity jurisdiction.

D. SMJ must be based on federal question jurisdiction.

2.  A  pedestrian was struck in a crosswalk by a car and was injured.
    Their hospital bill came to \$25,000, and they missed two months'
    work, losing \$20,000 of wages. The pedestrian also incurred pain
    and suffering. \
    \
    The pedestrian files a case in the lone federal court in their
    state, alleging that they suffered more than \$75,000 in damages. At
    trial, the jury awarded the pedestrian the requested damages plus
    \$24,000 in pain and suffering damages, for a total of \$69,000. \
    \
    Was there subject matter jurisdiction over the pedestrian's case? 

- \(A\) Yes, because the pedestrian alleged in good faith an amount in
  controversy exceeding \$75,000.

- \(B\) Yes, because the pedestrian\'s allegation as to the amount in
  controversy is taken at face value.

- \(C\) No, because the jury did not award the pedestrian an amount
  exceeding \$75,000.

- \(D\) No, because pain and suffering damages are not included when
  determining the amount in controversy.

3.  A pedestrian was injured when a car moved forward slightly and
    tapped the pedestrian without knocking them to the ground. The
    pedestrian was not taken to the hospital and incurred only \$5,000
    in medical bills.\
    \
    The pedestrian is domiciled in Ohio, but they were doing errands in
    preparation for their permanent move to Michigan. The pedestrian
    decided to put off their move to Michigan until the matter was
    resolved.\
    \
    The driver of the truck was domiciled in Ohio. \
    \
    If the court dismisses the case for a lack of subject matter
    jurisdiction, which of the following presents the most likely
    reason? 

    (a) The pedestrian and the driver are citizens of Ohio, but the
        amount in controversy is not relevant.

> \(b\) The amount in controversy is insufficient for diversity of
> citizenship jurisdiction, but the pedestrian would be considered a
> citizen of Michigan because they intended to move there permanently.
>
> \(C\) Federal courts cannot hear cases based on state law.
>
> \(D\) The amount in controversy is insufficient for diversity of
> citizenship jurisdiction, and the complete diversity requirement has
> not been satisfied.

**Answers**

1.  \(B\) is correct. This is an example of a case that satisfies both
    federal question and diversity jurisdiction. As such, it is one that
    is not solely based on diversity jurisdiction.\
    \
    (A) is incorrect because federal courts only have exclusive
    jurisdiction over a narrow range of cases. State courts generally
    can hear cases even though the case is based on a federal statute.\
    \
    (C) and (D) are incorrect because subject matter jurisdiction need
    not be based on one basis. Subject matter jurisdiction can overlap. 

2.  \(A\) is correct. The amount in controversy was sufficient because
    the amount likely was alleged in good faith. Pain and suffering
    damages are common in tort cases, and it's conceivable that a jury
    could have awarded the pedestrian an amount that would push the
    amount in controversy over \$75,000. Indeed, even if the jury
    declined to award any pain and suffering damages at all, the amount
    in controversy would have been satisfied so long as the damages were
    alleged in good faith. (Note that, if a defendant believes the
    amount in controversy is insufficient, they should bring the matter
    to the court's attention by motion well before trial.)\
    \
    (B) is incorrect. The plaintiff must plead in good faith that
    damages exceed \$75,000. The defendant may challenge the amount in
    controversy by showing that the plaintiff, to a legal certainty,
    cannot recover more than \$75,000. This will be difficult
    to prove in a torts case with pain and suffering damages, as those
    damages are not easily reduced to a specific figure.\
    \
    (C) is incorrect. An award of an amount that does not satisfy the
    amount in controversy does not retroactively defeat subject matter
    jurisdiction.\
    \
    (D) is incorrect. All damages are included when determining the
    amount in controversy. 

3.  D. is correct, Diversity of citizenship requires both an amount in
    controversy exceeding \$75,000, and there must be complete
    diversity, meaning that no plaintiff may share state citizenship
    with any defendant.\
    \
    An individual is a citizen of the state in which they are domiciled;
    one's domicile can be changed when the party is physically present
    in the new state with the intent to remain there permanently.\
    \
    Here, the amount in controversy is not satisfied, as it does not
    exceed \$75,000. Furthermore, the pedestrian has yet to change their
    domicile. Although they have the intent to remain permanently in
    Michigan eventually, they are not yet physically present in
    Michigan. Thus, the pedestrian's citizenship remains Ohio until the
    time they actually complete the move to Michigan. Accordingly, (A)
    and (B) are incorrect.\
    \
    (C) is incorrect because federal courts can hear cases based on
    state law under its diversity of citizenship jurisdiction. 

**Venue**

**1. Introduction to Venue**

Venue does not refer to a social location like where a party or event is
held. Instead, venue refers to the rules governing where a case can be
brought in federal court. Venue determines which federal district court
is the proper location for a case. Before discussing venue, it is
helpful to recall how a case gets into federal court in the first place.
A case can enter federal court in one of two ways. First, the case may
arise under federal law. Second, the parties may be citizens of
different states and the amount in controversy must exceed \$75,000. In
cases based on diversity jurisdiction, the federal district court
applies the substantive law of the state in which it sits.

The rules of subject matter jurisdiction determine whether a case can be
brought in federal court, while the rules of venue determine where
within the federal system the case should be brought.

**2. General Rule for Venue**

When a plaintiff initially files a case, the plaintiff may choose a
federal district in one of two ways:

1.  Residential venue

2.  Transactional venue

**3. Residential Venue**

Residential venue means that venue is proper in a federal district court
where any one defendant resides. However, all defendants must reside in
the same state for residential venue to apply. The defendants do not
need to reside in the same district within that state, but they must all
reside in the same state.

> **A. Human Defendants**
>
> Human defendants reside in the federal district where they are
> domiciled, meaning where they have their permanent home.
>
> **For example**, if one defendant resides in the Southern District of
> California and another resides in the Central District of California,
> residential venue would be proper in either district because both
> defendants reside in California. However, if one defendant resides in
> the Southern District of California and another resides in the
> Northern District of Illinois, residential venue would not be proper
> because the defendants reside in different states.
>
> **B. Business Defendants**
>
> Business defendants reside in any state in which they are subject to
> personal jurisdiction.
>
> This includes both general personal jurisdiction and specific personal
> jurisdiction.
>
> Once personal jurisdiction exists over a business, venue is also
> proper for that business.

**4. Transactional Venue**

Transactional venue allows a plaintiff to file a case in a federal
district where a substantial part of the claim arose or where a
substantial part of the property involved in the case is located. If a
substantial part of the claim arose in more than one district, then any
of those districts is a proper venue.

> **For example**, if a defective product was manufactured in one state
> and caused injury in another state, a federal district court in either
> location would be a proper venue.

**5. Fallback Rule for Venue**

If the defendants reside in different states and no substantial part of
the claim arose in any district, such as when a claim arises outside the
United States, then venue is proper in any district where any defendant
is subject to personal jurisdiction.

**6. Transfer of Venue**

In some cases, the plaintiff may file in an improper district, or the
defendant may object to the chosen venue.

In those situations, the case may be transferred to another federal
district court.

A federal district court may transfer a case to another federal district
court as long as the receiving court is a proper venue and has personal
jurisdiction over the defendant.

A federal court cannot transfer a case to a state court or to a foreign
court based on venue rules.

> **A. If Venue Was Improper**
>
> If the original district was improperly chosen, the court may:

- Transfer the case to a proper district, or

- Dismiss the case

> Courts generally prefer to transfer the case if possible.
>
> **B. If Venue Was Proper**
>
> If the original district was properly chosen, the court may still
> transfer the case to another proper district.
>
> The party requesting the transfer, usually the defendant, has the
> burden of showing that transfer is appropriate.
>
> **C. Factors Considered in Transfer**
>
> When deciding whether to transfer a case, the court considers both
> public and private factors.
>
> **Public factors include:**

- Which law applies

- Which community should bear the burden of jury service

> **Private factors include:**

- Convenience for the parties

- Ease of presenting evidence

**7. Law Applied After Transfer (Erie Doctrine)**

In diversity cases, a federal court applies the substantive law of the
state in which it sits.

After a transfer, the applicable law depends on whether the original
venue was proper.

> **A. If Original Venue Was Proper**
>
> If the original venue was proper, then the substantive law of the
> original forum continues to apply after transfer.
>
> **B. If Original Venue Was Improper**
>
> If the original venue was improper, then the court applies the law of
> the state to which the case is transferred.
>
> This rule prevents plaintiffs from engaging in forum shopping, which
> is the practice of filing in an improper forum to gain a legal
> advantage.
>
> **C. Exception: Forum Selection Clause**
>
> A forum selection clause is an agreement between parties specifying
> where disputes will be litigated.
>
> If such a clause exists and a party files suit in a different
> location, the opposing party may seek to enforce the clause by
> requesting transfer.
>
> In cases involving a valid forum selection clause:

- Courts consider only public factors, not private convenience factors

- The substantive law of the selected forum applies

**8. Forum Non Conveniens**

Forum non conveniens is a doctrine that applies when a case is filed in
federal court but another court in a different judicial system is the
more appropriate location.

This often arises in international or multi-jurisdictional cases.

Because a federal court cannot transfer a case to a court in a different
judicial system, it may dismiss the case under forum non conveniens.

> **A. Factors Considered**
>
> The court considers similar public and private factors used in venue
> transfer.
>
> Additionally, the court considers whether the alternative forum is
> adequate.
>
> **B. Adequacy of Alternative Forum**
>
> An alternative forum is generally considered adequate unless the
> plaintiff would have no available remedy there.
>
> The court may consider whether the alternative forum allows:

- Jury trials

- Recovery for pain and suffering

- Other remedies

**9. Conclusion**

Venue determines the proper federal district court where a case may be
brought.

The rules governing venue include residential venue, transactional
venue, and fallback provisions. Courts also have the authority to
transfer cases between federal districts or dismiss them when another
forum is more appropriate. The doctrines of transfer, forum selection
clauses, and forum non conveniens ensure that cases are heard in
appropriate and fair locations.

**Personal jurisdiction**

**Civil Procedure -- Personal Jurisdiction (Organized Transcript Notes
in Complete Sentences)**

**1. Introduction to Personal Jurisdiction**

Personal jurisdiction refers to the court's power over the parties in a
case.

Subject matter jurisdiction involves a court's power to hear a
particular type of case, while personal jurisdiction focuses on whether
the court has authority over the defendant.

Because the plaintiff files the case, the court automatically has power
over the plaintiff based on consent.

Consent is a valid basis for personal jurisdiction, and if a party
consents to jurisdiction, the court may exercise authority over them.

The primary focus of personal jurisdiction analysis is on situations
where the defendant does not consent and the court must determine
whether it can require the defendant to appear and be bound by its
judgment.

**2. Basic Principle of Personal Jurisdiction**

A defendant cannot be brought into any court in any state without
sufficient justification.

The central question is whether the defendant has sufficient contacts
with the forum state such that exercising jurisdiction would be fair and
reasonable.

**3. Two-Step Analysis for Personal Jurisdiction**

Both federal and state courts apply a two-step analysis:

1.  The exercise of jurisdiction must fall within a state statute.

2.  The exercise of jurisdiction must satisfy the Constitution's Due
    Process Clause.

**4. Step One: State Long-Arm Statute**

Each state has a long-arm statute that determines when courts may
exercise jurisdiction over nonresidents.

Some states have broad long-arm statutes that extend jurisdiction to the
full limits allowed by the Constitution.

Other states have more limited statutes that list specific situations
where jurisdiction is allowed, such as:

- Committing a tort in the state

- Entering into a contract in the state

- Owning property in the state

The case must arise from one of the listed contacts if the statute is
limited.

**5. Step Two: Constitutional Due Process (Minimum Contacts)**

After confirming that a state statute allows jurisdiction, the court
must determine whether exercising jurisdiction satisfies due process.

This requires analyzing whether the defendant has sufficient minimum
contacts with the forum state such that jurisdiction would be fair.

This analysis consists of three components:

1.  Contact (purposeful availment)

2.  Relatedness

3.  Fairness

**6. Purposeful Availment (Contact Requirement)**

The defendant must have purposefully established contact with the forum
state.

This means the defendant must have voluntarily reached out to the state
to take advantage of its privileges, protections, or benefits.

For example:

- Delivering goods to a state constitutes purposeful availment.

- Purchasing property in a state also constitutes purposeful availment.

The focus is not on whether the harmful act was intentional, but whether
the defendant's connection to the state was purposeful.

**Example: David and Maya**

David lives in California and takes a ferry ride that crosses into New
York waters.

While in New York waters, David negligently injures Maya.

David has purposefully availed himself of New York because he knowingly
entered the state and benefited from its protections.

**7. Foreseeability**

The defendant's conduct must make it foreseeable that they could be sued
in the forum state.

This means the defendant must have known or reasonably anticipated that
their activities in the state could subject them to litigation there.

**8. Relatedness**

The plaintiff's claim must arise out of or relate to the defendant's
contact with the forum.

In the example of David and Maya, the injury occurred in New York and
was caused by David's actions in New York, so the claim is directly
related to his contact with the state.

Even brief contact with the state can satisfy this requirement if the
claim arises from that contact.

**9. Fairness (Reasonableness)**

The court must determine whether exercising jurisdiction is fair and
reasonable.

This involves evaluating the nature and quality of the defendant's
contacts with the state.

**Fairness Factors**

1.  **Burden on the defendant and witnesses**\
    The forum does not need to be convenient, but it must not place the
    defendant at a severe or grave disadvantage.

2.  **State's interest**\
    The state has an interest in providing a forum for resolving
    disputes, especially when harm occurs within the state.

3.  **Plaintiff's interest**\
    The plaintiff's choice of forum is given substantial weight and is
    not easily overturned.

**Example: Danny and Priya**

Danny, from California, causes a car accident in Florida that injures
Priya, who is from New York.

Danny's actions in Florida create sufficient contacts, and the claim
arises from those contacts.

Florida has an interest in enforcing its laws and providing a forum, and
litigating there does not place Danny at a severe disadvantage.

Therefore, exercising jurisdiction in Florida is fair and reasonable.

**10. Specific vs. General Personal Jurisdiction**

**A. Specific Personal Jurisdiction**

Specific jurisdiction exists when the plaintiff's claim arises out of
the defendant's contact with the forum state.

All of the prior analysis applies to specific jurisdiction.

**B. General Personal Jurisdiction**

General jurisdiction exists when the defendant is "at home" in the forum
state.

For individuals, this means the state of domicile.

For corporations, this means:

- The state of incorporation

- The principal place of business

If general jurisdiction exists, the defendant can be sued in that state
for any claim, even if the claim did not arise from activities in that
state.

**Example: Aisha**

Aisha is domiciled in Arizona.

Even if she causes injury abroad, she can still be sued in Arizona
because she is "at home" there and subject to general jurisdiction.

**11. Transient Jurisdiction (Service in the State)**

A court may exercise jurisdiction over a defendant who is served with
process while physically present in the state.

This applies even if the defendant is only temporarily in the state.

**Example: Jo at the Airport**

Jo, who is domiciled in Maine, is served with process while at an
airport in New York during a layover.

New York courts may exercise jurisdiction over Jo based on service
within the state.

**12. Conclusion**

Personal jurisdiction determines whether a court has authority over a
defendant.

It requires both a statutory basis and compliance with constitutional
due process.

The key considerations include purposeful contact, relatedness, and
fairness.

In addition to specific jurisdiction, courts may exercise general
jurisdiction over defendants who are "at home" in the forum, or
jurisdiction based on service within the state.

**Complaint, Joinder, and Service (Organized Transcript Notes in
Complete Sentences)**

**1. Introduction**

A case in federal court begins with a complaint.

Once the plaintiff has determined that the court has subject matter
jurisdiction and that venue is proper, the plaintiff must file a
complaint to initiate the case.

**2. The Complaint**

A complaint must include three required elements:

1.  A statement of the grounds for subject matter jurisdiction

2.  A statement of the claim

3.  A demand for the relief sought

The complaint does not need to prove that the plaintiff will win.
Instead, it must provide enough facts to give notice to the defendant of
what the case is about and to show that the plaintiff has a plausible
claim.

The determination of plausibility is made by the judge using common
sense and experience.

**3. Rule 11 Certification**

Under Rule 11 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, all pleadings and
papers must include a certification that:

- The filing is not being presented for an improper purpose

- The legal arguments are warranted by existing law or a reasonable
  extension of the law

- The factual contentions have evidentiary support

- Any denials are supported by evidence or a lack of information

The plaintiff is typically responsible for making this certification
because the plaintiff is the party with knowledge of the facts.

Violations of Rule 11 can result in sanctions imposed on the attorney,
the attorney's law firm, and even the represented party.

**4. Joinder of Claims**

Joinder refers to what claims may be included in a single lawsuit.

A plaintiff may join any claims they have against a single defendant,
even if the claims are unrelated, as long as the court has subject
matter jurisdiction.

**A. Aggregation of Claims**

A single plaintiff may add together the value of all claims against a
single defendant to meet the amount in controversy requirement for
diversity jurisdiction.

The claims do not need to be related, and no individual claim needs to
exceed \$75,000 as long as the total exceeds that amount.

**Example: Maya and Aisha**

Maya, a resident of New York, sues Aisha, a resident of Arizona, for:

- Breach of contract

- Defamation

- Repayment of a personal loan

Although these claims are unrelated, Maya may join all of them in one
lawsuit, and their combined value can satisfy the amount in controversy
requirement.

**5. Permissive Joinder of Parties**

Permissive joinder allows multiple parties to be joined in one lawsuit
under certain conditions.

Two requirements must be satisfied:

1.  Joinder must be authorized by rule

2.  There must be subject matter jurisdiction

A party may be joined if:

- The claim arises out of the same transaction or occurrence (or series
  of transactions or occurrences), and

- There is at least one common question of law or fact

**Example: Maya, Aisha, and Tia (Car Accident)**

If Maya attempts to add Tia as a defendant based on a separate car
accident, this joinder is not permitted because the claim does not arise
from the same transaction or occurrence as the claims against Aisha.

Therefore, Maya must bring that claim separately.

**Example: Maya, Aisha, and Tia (Defamation)**

If Tia participates in the same defamatory conduct as Aisha, then Maya
may join Tia as a defendant because the claim arises from the same
transaction or occurrence and shares common legal and factual issues.

Subject matter jurisdiction would also be satisfied if diversity and the
amount in controversy requirements are met.

**6. Joinder of Plaintiffs**

The same permissive joinder rules apply to plaintiffs.

Multiple plaintiffs may join together if their claims arise from the
same transaction or occurrence and share common questions of law or
fact.

**7. Compulsory Joinder (Required Parties)**

Compulsory joinder refers to situations where certain parties must be
included in a lawsuit.

**A. Determining Necessity**

An absent party is considered necessary if:

- Complete relief cannot be provided without them

- Their absence would impair their ability to protect their interests

- Their absence would expose existing parties to multiple or
  inconsistent obligations

**B. Feasibility of Joinder**

If a party is necessary, the court must determine whether it is feasible
to join them.

Joinder is feasible if:

- The court has personal jurisdiction over the party

- The court has subject matter jurisdiction over the claim

**C. If Joinder Is Not Feasible**

If a necessary party cannot be joined, the court must decide whether to
proceed without them or dismiss the case.

The court considers:

- Whether an alternative forum exists

- The likelihood of harm to the absent party

- Whether the court can shape relief to avoid harm

**D. Example: Tia as a Necessary Party**

In the scenario involving Aisha and Tia, Tia is not a necessary party
because:

- Complete relief can be granted without her

- She is not bound by a judgment against Aisha

- She can litigate her own involvement separately

**8. Service of Process**

After filing the complaint, the plaintiff must notify the defendant of
the lawsuit through service of process.

**A. Components of Process**

Process consists of two documents:

1.  The summons, which notifies the defendant of the lawsuit and the
    time to respond

2.  A copy of the complaint

Providing these documents ensures that the defendant receives proper
notice, which is required by the Constitution.

**B. Who May Serve Process**

Process may be served by any person who:

- Is at least 18 years old

- Is not a party to the case

**C. Methods of Service**

**1. Personal Service**

The defendant may be personally served anywhere.

**2. Substituted Service**

Substituted service is allowed only if:

- It occurs at the defendant's usual place of abode, and

- It is made on someone of suitable age and discretion who resides there

**Example: Dog Sitter vs. Roommate**

A dog sitter who does not live at the residence cannot accept service.

An adult roommate who lives at the residence can accept substituted
service.

**3. Service on Businesses**

Process may be served on:

- An officer of the business

- An authorized agent

**4. State Law Methods**

Federal courts also allow service methods permitted by state law where:

- The court sits, or

- Service is made

Many states allow service by mail.

**9. Waiver of Service**

A defendant may waive formal service of process.

**A. Procedure**

The plaintiff sends a request for waiver to the defendant.

If the defendant signs and returns the waiver within the required time
(at least 30 days), service is waived.

**B. Incentives for Waiver**

1.  **Cost**\
    If the defendant refuses to waive service without good reason, they
    must pay the cost of service.

2.  **Time**\
    A defendant who waives service has 60 days to respond instead of 21
    days.

**10. Proof of Service**

After service is completed:

- If service is waived, the plaintiff files the waiver with the court

- If service is not waived, the process server files a report detailing
  how service was made

**11. Conclusion**

A federal case begins with the filing of a complaint that satisfies
pleading requirements and Rule 11.

The rules of joinder determine what claims and parties may or must be
included.

Finally, service of process ensures that the defendant receives proper
notice of the lawsuit, allowing the case to proceed.

**Civil Procedure -- Defendant's Response (Organized Transcript Notes in
Complete Sentences)**

**1. Introduction: Being Served and the Need to Respond**

A case begins for the defendant when they are served with a summons and
complaint.

Once a defendant is served, they must respond to the plaintiff's
complaint in federal court.

Unlike informal responses in everyday life, a defendant's response in
federal court must follow specific procedural rules found in Rule 12 of
the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure.

**2. Timing of the Response**

If a defendant is served with process, they must respond within 21 days.

If the defendant waives service of process, they have 60 days from the
date the waiver request was mailed to respond.

If the defendant fails to respond within the required time, the court
may enter a default judgment against them.

**3. Types of Responses**

A defendant may respond to a complaint in one of two ways:

1.  By filing a pre-answer motion

2.  By filing an answer

**4. Pre-Answer Motions (Rule 12 Motions)**

A motion is a request for a court order.

A defendant may file a pre-answer motion if they want the court to
address problems with the complaint before responding to its
allegations.

**A. Motions Addressing Defects in the Complaint**

If the complaint is too vague or ambiguous for the defendant to
reasonably respond, the defendant may file a **motion for a more
definite statement**.

If the complaint contains redundant, immaterial, or irrelevant
information, the defendant may file a **motion to strike** those
portions.

**Example**

If a complaint includes vague allegations such as "the defendant tricked
me" without details, the defendant may request a more definite
statement.

If the complaint includes irrelevant allegations, such as unrelated
personal criticisms, the defendant may move to strike those statements.

**B. Motions to Dismiss (Rule 12(b) Defenses)**

Before filing an answer, the defendant may also file a motion to dismiss
based on certain defenses.

**1. Defenses That Must Be Raised First (Waived if Not Raised)**

The following defenses must be raised in the defendant's first response
(either in a motion or in the answer), or they are waived:

- Lack of personal jurisdiction

- Improper venue

- Insufficient process

- Insufficient service of process

**2. Defenses That Can Be Raised Later**

The following defenses may be raised in the first response but are not
waived if omitted:

- Failure to state a claim upon which relief can be granted

- Failure to join a required (indispensable) party

- Lack of subject matter jurisdiction

Failure to state a claim and failure to join a required party may be
raised as late as trial.

Lack of subject matter jurisdiction may be raised at any time because a
court without subject matter jurisdiction lacks authority over the case.

**C. Effect of Filing a Motion**

If the defendant files a Rule 12 motion and the court denies it, the
defendant must then file an answer.

**5. The Answer**

An answer is a pleading in which the defendant responds to the
allegations in the complaint and may raise affirmative defenses.

**A. Ways to Respond to Allegations**

A defendant may respond to each allegation by:

1.  Admitting the allegation

2.  Denying the allegation

3.  Stating that they lack sufficient knowledge to admit or deny

A statement of insufficient knowledge has the same effect as a denial,
but it may only be used when the information is not reasonably available
to the defendant.

**Examples**

A defendant cannot claim lack of knowledge about facts within their
control, such as whether their driver's license was suspended.

However, a defendant may claim lack of knowledge regarding matters
outside their control, such as the extent of the plaintiff's injuries.

**B. Effect of Failing to Deny**

If a defendant fails to deny an allegation, it is treated as an
admission, except with respect to the amount of damages.

**Example**

If the defendant responds that the plaintiff "cannot prove" an
allegation rather than denying it, the statement is treated as an
admission.

**C. Affirmative Defenses**

In the answer, the defendant may assert affirmative defenses, which
introduce new facts that could defeat the plaintiff's claim.

Examples of affirmative defenses include:

- Statute of limitations

- Statute of frauds

- Res judicata

- Self-defense

Defenses that could have been raised in a motion may also be included as
affirmative defenses in the answer.

Failure to raise affirmative defenses may result in waiver.

**6. Counterclaims**

A counterclaim is a claim brought by the defendant against the
plaintiff.

**A. Compulsory Counterclaims**

A compulsory counterclaim arises out of the same transaction or
occurrence as the plaintiff's claim.

The defendant must raise a compulsory counterclaim in the current case
or risk waiving it, unless it has already been filed in another case.

**Example**

If the plaintiff sues for breach of contract, and the defendant claims
the plaintiff actually breached the contract, that claim is compulsory.

**B. Permissive Counterclaims**

A permissive counterclaim does not arise out of the same transaction or
occurrence.

The defendant may bring a permissive counterclaim, but is not required
to do so.

The court must have subject matter jurisdiction over the permissive
counterclaim.

**7. Rule 11 Certification (Revisited)**

All filings in federal court, including answers and counterclaims, must
be certified by the attorney or unrepresented party.

This certification confirms that:

- The filing is not for an improper purpose

- The legal claims are supported by law or a reasonable extension

- The factual statements are true or supported by evidence

**8. Conclusion**

A defendant must respond to a complaint within the required time by
filing either a motion or an answer.

Pre-answer motions allow the defendant to challenge defects in the
complaint or raise certain defenses.

An answer addresses the allegations and may include affirmative defenses
and counterclaims.

Failure to properly respond or raise defenses may result in waiver or
default judgment.

**MBE Strategy -- Systematic Problem Solving (Organized Transcript Notes
in Complete Sentences)**

**1. Main Goal of the Workshop**

One of the main goals of this workshop is to teach students how to
systematically answer MBE questions.

The goal is to learn and practice a system that allows you, more often
than not, to predict the right answer before looking at the four answer
choices.

When you turn to the answer choices, you should already have an answer
in mind and look for the choice that best matches your prediction.

Sometimes your predicted answer will be precise, and other times it will
be less exact.

**2. Three Areas Needed for Bar Exam Success**

Success on the bar exam requires competency in three areas:

1.  A strong command of substantive legal rules

2.  Strong reading comprehension skills

3.  Strong systematic problem-solving habits

These three areas work like the legs of a stool. If one leg is weak, the
whole stool may collapse.

**3. How Multiple Choice Questions Are Built**

Unlike essay questions, which may require spotting and analyzing
multiple issues, a multiple choice question tests a narrow point of law.

Only one issue really matters.

A multiple choice question must have one correct answer and three
incorrect answers, so the question must be answerable in a single
sentence that predicts the outcome of the issue that matters.

A question may appear to raise several issues, but the outcome usually
turns on one narrow legal point.

**Example: Negligence**

A multiple choice question cannot broadly test all of negligence at
once, including duty, breach, causation, and damages.

Instead, it will test one narrow rule within negligence, such as:

- The duty of care owed by a child

- The duty of care owed by a doctor

- The duty of care owed during an emergency

**The Dartboard Image**

A multiple choice question can be thought of like a dartboard.

The narrow issue being tested is the bullseye.

The goal is to reach that center point by identifying the precise legal
rule that controls the answer.

The facts and the call of the question will not always tell you directly
what the narrow issue is, so you must work toward identifying it.

**4. How Questions Test Substantive Knowledge**

MBE questions do not usually ask for a rule directly.

Instead, they require you to read a legal problem, make connections
based on your substantive knowledge, and identify the rule needed to
resolve the problem.

As you read, your brain should associate facts and words from the
question with legal rules you have learned.

The call of the question is the question that follows the fact pattern
and tells you what you must answer.

Your success depends on whether you know enough rules and whether your
understanding of those rules is accurate.

**Example: Contracts and the UCC**

If a hypothetical involves a sale of goods, the UCC applies.

Although the mirror image rule might come to mind, that is a common law
doctrine.

The better association is the battle of the forms provision under the
UCC, which helps determine whether a reply is an acceptance and whether
terms become part of the contract.

**5. Systematic MBE Problem-Solving Strategy**

The endpoint of the strategy is to identify and analyze the narrow issue
presented so that you can predict the correct answer before reading the
answer choices.

**Step 1: Read the Call of the Question First**

The first step is to begin every question by reading the call of the
question first.

This may feel counterintuitive because people are used to reading from
top to bottom.

However, the call often provides important information about the
subject, subtopic, and legal issue being tested.

**Step 2: Question the Call**

After reading the call, pause and ask yourself what associations came to
mind.

You should ask what additional information you need before you can
answer the call.

These become "research questions" that guide your reading of the facts.

Examples of research questions include:

- What else do I need to know?

- What facts am I looking for?

- What legal rule might be involved?

This step takes practice but provides significant benefits.

**Step 3: Actively Read the Facts**

Next, actively read the hypothetical while searching for answers to your
research questions.

As you read, you should circle, underline, take notes, or draw small
charts when helpful.

You should identify facts that trigger legal rules and note any new
questions that arise.

**Step 4: Step Back and Analyze**

After reading the facts, step back and think through what you have read.

You should pull together the associations you formed and the answers to
your research questions.

Then return to the call of the question and ask whether you can identify
the narrow issue being tested.

The goal is to determine the specific rule that controls the outcome.

**Step 5: Predict the Answer Before Looking at Choices**

Before reading the answer choices, answer the call of the question in
your head.

Many calls invite answers such as:

- Yes or no

- Constitutional or unconstitutional

- Admissible or inadmissible

- Recover or not recover

You should not stop with a conclusion. You should add a "because" clause
explaining the legal reason.

For example, you might think, "Yes, the defendant will prevail because
the plaintiff's injury was not proximately caused by the defendant's
breach."

Or you might think, "Yes, the statement is admissible because the dying
declaration exception applies."

**Step 6: Match Your Prediction to the Answer Choices**

After predicting the answer, turn to the answer choices.

Select the answer choice that best matches your predicted answer.

This helps prevent incorrect answer choices from misleading you.

**6. Why Reading the Call First Matters**

Reading the call first helps you identify the subject, subtopic, and
legally significant facts.

Sometimes the facts may suggest one area of law, but the call may reveal
that the question is actually testing something else.

**Example: Intentional Torts vs. Negligence**

A fact pattern may include facts suggesting assault, battery, or
transferred intent.

However, the call of the question may ask about negligence.

If you read the call first, you know to focus on negligence and avoid
being distracted by intentional tort facts.

This type of misleading setup is called a "head fake."

**Distractor Facts**

Distractor facts are facts included in the question that are not legally
significant to the issue being tested.

They may not be irrelevant in a general sense, but they do not affect
the answer to the specific call of the question.

Reading the call first helps you identify and ignore distractors.

**7. Using the Call to Form Associations**

The call of the question can often reveal the subject or issue being
tested.

For example, if the call asks which standard a court should use to
determine the validity of an ordinance, that suggests a constitutional
law question involving levels of scrutiny.

From there, you should ask research questions such as:

- What is the basis for the suit?

- What does the ordinance say?

- What part of the Constitution is implicated?

These questions help you read the facts with a purpose.

**Naked Calls**

Sometimes the call of the question gives very little information, such
as "The court should" or "The court will most likely."

When the call is vague, read the sentence immediately before the call.

If that still does not help, glance at the answer choices to determine
the subject or subtopic being tested.

**8. Active Reading and Marking the Facts**

Systematic problem-solving requires active reading.

You should underline, circle, annotate, or mark facts that answer your
research questions or trigger legal associations.

Because the MBE includes 100 questions in 3 hours, twice in one day, you
must help your brain process information efficiently.

Marking the question helps you identify important words and phrases and
improves reading comprehension.

**9. Identifying the Narrow Issue**

After reading the facts, step back and analyze the question.

There may be several issues to think through, but only one narrow issue
will usually control the outcome.

The narrow issue is the point that changes the answer from yes to no,
constitutional to unconstitutional, admissible to inadmissible, or
prevail to not prevail.

When you identify that issue, you have reached the bullseye.

**10. Why Predicting Before Looking at Answers Matters**

Predicting the answer before reading the answer choices is the most
important step.

If you look at the answer choices without a strong idea of the correct
result, the incorrect choices can mislead you.

Incorrect answer choices often rely on distractor facts from the
hypothetical.

If you do not identify the narrow issue and predict the answer first,
you become vulnerable to attractive but wrong answer choices.

**Protecting Yourself from Distractors**

The key to answering MBE questions correctly is to approach each
question with a goal.

That goal is to identify the narrow issue being tested while protecting
yourself from distractor facts and misleading answer choices.

The systematic approach helps you keep your eye on the bullseye and
avoid obstacles throughout the question.

**11. Final Summary**

MBE questions test narrow points of law.

To answer them effectively, you should read the call first, question the
call, actively read the facts, step back to identify the narrow issue,
predict the answer, and then choose the answer that best matches your
prediction.

This process strengthens rule recall, reading comprehension, and
problem-solving habits.
