## The Pattern Has a Name: Authority Saturation

The phenomenon Javier is describing is documented in psychology under several related frameworks:

### Dominance Depletion / Role Saturation

Individuals who occupy high-authority roles — managers, leaders, developers carrying technical ownership, people who are the decision-maker in multiple domains of their daily life — frequently experience what researchers describe as **dominance depletion**: the motivational and neurological resources required to maintain authority are finite, and when chronically exercised, the drive to continue exercising them in *additional* contexts diminishes sharply.

This is not weakness. It is the logical output of a nervous system that has been running the dominance circuitry — prefrontal control, executive decision-making, social authority management — at high capacity all day. The brain's reward for sustained dominance is status and control. When you already have that in abundance, the marginal reward of *more dominance* approaches zero.

Conversely, the reward signal for *surrender of control* in someone who is chronically in authority is extremely high. The nervous system is essentially craving the opposite of what it runs on all day.

### The Research Basis

Leary et al. (1994) and subsequent work on the **Dominance Behavioral System (DBS)** established that dominance motivation operates on a satiation curve — like hunger, it builds when deprived and diminishes when satisfied. People in high-status daily environments frequently show lower dominance motivation in recreational and intimate contexts precisely because the drive is already being met through other channels.

Additionally, studies on **sexual role preference and personality** (Wismeijer & Van Assen, 2013) found that individuals scoring high on *conscientiousness* and who occupy leadership roles in daily life are overrepresented in the submissive preference category — not because they are passive personalities, but because the appeal of genuine surrender is highest in those for whom it is most rare.

The colloquial version of this is well-known in D/s communities: the high-powered executive who is a submissive. It is not a contradiction. It is a direct and logical extension of who they are.

## Three Paths Forward

### Path 1: Reframe the Dynamic Entirely

If Javier's natural pull is toward submission or neutral, and his partner is genuinely a switch (oscillates between dominant and submissive), the most honest configuration might be:

- Partner takes the dominant role when they want to
- Javier explores what submission actually feels like for him on his own terms
- Both people get what they actually want instead of each performing what the other requested

This requires a frank out-of-scene conversation: *"I think I've been approaching this backwards. I want to understand what it's like from the other side."*

### Path 2: The Minimal Dominant Role

If Javier wants to try verbal dominance but in the simplest possible form, the research supports a low-effort, high-impact version:

- **Presence and attention** — genuine focused listening, noticing, and naming what you observe. This alone is a powerful dominant act.
- **One clear directive at a time** — not a complex scene structure, just a single calm instruction and holding it
- **Not performing a character** — being exactly who you are, but choosing to direct the interaction

The key insight: authentic calm authority from someone who already has it naturally is more effective than an elaborate performance. You don't have to become a different person. You carry authority naturally. The only shift is *choosing* to let it be present in this context, briefly, without it feeling like work.

### Path 3: Honest Pause

If neither of the above feels right — if the dominant role genuinely doesn't fit and the partner's oscillation makes the dynamic exhausting — the most self-respecting option is to name that clearly:

*"I'm not comfortable with this role right now. I need to understand my own preferences before I can reliably hold space for yours."*

That is not rejection. It is consent applied to yourself.

**What submission actually feels like for someone with Javier's profile:**

It is not weakness. It is the one context where the executive function can switch off — where someone else holds the space, makes the decisions, carries the authority, and you don't have to. The neurological relief of that for someone in chronic authority is significant. The research on subspace describes exactly that state — and nothing says that state is exclusive to people who identify as submissive by default. It is available to anyone whose nervous system is ready to stop running the dominance circuit for a while.
