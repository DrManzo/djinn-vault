# Introduction to Calculus

## Summary
This MIT page provides an introduction to calculus, explaining its core concepts and applications.

## Key Points
- **Core Idea of Calculus**: Study of change via "instantaneous change."
- **Why We Study It**: Enables building quantitative models of changing systems and understanding how to control them.
- **Course Structure**:
  - Functions: Position, speed, acceleration.
  - Derivatives: Finding instantaneous changes.
  - Applications: Optimization and modeling.
  - Integrals: Accumulated change.
  - Integration Methods: Techniques for integration.
  - Series: Infinite sums.

## Details
Calculus is described as the study of how things **change**, giving a framework to model systems that change and deduce quantitative predictions from those models. The fundamental idea is to study change via "instantaneous change," meaning changes over tiny time intervals, because these are often much simpler to model than changes over long intervals.

### Why We Study It
- **Modeling Changing Systems**: Calculus allows you to build relatively simple quantitative models of changing systems (physical, economic, biological, etc.) and then deduce their consequences.
- **Control and Power Over the Material World**: By understanding how changing conditions affect a system, you can learn how to control it to behave in desired ways. This is framed as a source of "extraordinary power over the material world," especially for engineers.

### Course Structure
For single-variable calculus (one input variable, like time):

1. **Functions**:
   - Position, speed, and acceleration are described using functions.
2. **Derivatives**:
   - Finding instantaneous change (the derivative) and rules for differentiation.
3. **Applications of f′f'f′**:
   - Optimizing and modeling where a function is increasing, decreasing, or at an extremum.
4. **Integrals**:
   - Using derivatives to solve problems and reconstructing functions from their derivatives (integration).
5. **Integration Methods**:
   - Techniques for integrating various classes of functions.
6. **Using Integration for Geometric Problems**:
   - Areas and volumes.

### Additional Topics
- Power series representations of functions and the study of convergence of infinite series.

## Numbers, Functions, and Operations
The introductory chapter revisits number systems: starting from natural numbers, then extending via subtraction, division, and square roots to negative numbers, rational numbers, and complex numbers. Decimal expansions are used to present real numbers and connect to countability. The functions chapter begins with an abstract definition of a function as a set of argument–value pairs, then builds up "standard functions" from the identity function and the exponential function.
