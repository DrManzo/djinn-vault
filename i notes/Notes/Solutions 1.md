## Solutions for Living Arrangements and Biopsychosocial Well-Being

### Emerging Adulthood

**Living Arrangement 1: Living with College Roommates**

- **Emotional:** Mortimer, J. T., Kim, Y.-J., & Swartz, H. L. (2010). *The transition to adulthood*. Journal of Marriage and Family, 72(3), 549-568. Living with roommates can provide emotional support and reduce feelings of loneliness, which is crucial during the transition to adulthood. This arrangement fosters social connections and enhances emotional resilience among young adults.

- **Social:** Hughes, M., & Gove, W. (1981). *Loneliness in later life: A longitudinal study*. Journal of Gerontology, 36(2), 174-180. Sharing living space encourages social interaction, collaboration, and the development of interpersonal skills. It also helps young adults build a network of friends that can be beneficial for future career opportunities.

- **Physical:** Mortimer et al. (2010). Living with roommates may influence each other's health behaviors positively, such as exercising together or cooking healthy meals. However, they may also contribute to unhealthy habits, like late-night eating or irregular sleep patterns.

- **Financial:** Mortimer et al. (2010). Sharing rent and utilities can alleviate financial strain, allowing young adults to allocate resources to education or savings. However, reliance on roommates may hinder the development of financial independence.

**Living Arrangement 2: Living Alone**

- **Emotional:** Hughes & Gove (1981). Living alone can lead to increased feelings of loneliness and depression, especially if social support networks are weak. However, it can also foster independence and self-discovery.

- **Social:** Hughes & Gove (1981). Individuals living alone may experience social isolation, which can negatively impact their mental health. The lack of daily interaction can lead to a decrease in social skills over time.

- **Physical:** Hughes & Gove (1981). Those living alone may struggle with maintaining a healthy lifestyle due to the absence of social encouragement. They might be less motivated to engage in physical activities or prepare nutritious meals.

- **Financial:** Hughes & Gove (1981). Financial independence is a significant aspect of living alone; however, it may lead to financial strain if income is insufficient.
