# Workplace Accountability — Estimate Error Conflict

## Summary
A workplace conflict over an estimate error where the subject created an estimate using the boss's notes, which contained an incorrect part. The boss wants to place sole blame on the subject, who refuses to accept full accountability for a shared oversight. The subject acknowledges they should have double-checked the part against the notes but argues the boss also had opportunity to review the drawings and estimate before sending to the customer.

## Key Points
- Subject created estimate using boss's notes; notes contained incorrect part specification
- Subject also created drawing and 3D model showing the part used — visual documentation existed
- Boss's position: estimate should be automatically correct on review; subject's position: that's unrealistic
- Subject acknowledges they should have double-checked the part but refuses sole blame
- Workplace culture issue: told to "expect the customer is stupid" but then labeled stupid themselves when errors occur
- Subject's approach: thorough documentation for installers (110% certainty) because of past experiences being burned
- ADHD mentioned but not used as crutch — subject takes pride in accuracy and thoroughness
- Core principle: accountability should be shared when multiple people are involved in a process
- Proposed solution: mandatory double-checks, improved communication protocols, standardized checklists

## Details
The conflict reveals deeper workplace dynamics around blame culture versus learning culture. The subject's thoroughness (drawings, 3D models, detailed documentation) demonstrates commitment to quality, but one missed part becomes the focal point for disproportionate blame. The boss's expectation that estimates should be "automatically correct" on review is unrealistic and sets up a no-win dynamic. The subject's refusal to accept sole blame is grounded in a reasonable principle: when a process involves multiple checkpoints, failure at any checkpoint is a shared responsibility. The frustration with being told to "write everything out for the stupid customer" and then being called stupid themselves highlights a hypocritical communication culture. The proposed improvements (double-checks, checklists, collaborative reviews) shift focus from blame to system improvement.
