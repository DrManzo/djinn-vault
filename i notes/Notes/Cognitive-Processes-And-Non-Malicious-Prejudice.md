# Cognitive Processes and Non-Malicious Prejudice

## Summary
Prejudice is often the result of cognitive processes rather than intentional malice. This note explores how cognitive biases, such as categorization and heuristics, can lead to biased judgments without malicious intent.

## Key Points
- Cognitive processes like categorization and reliance on heuristics can produce prejudice.
- Examples include confirmation bias and social learning that entrench stereotypes.
- A personal example of non-malicious prejudice involves favoring applicants from one's alma mater due to cognitive preference for familiarity.

## Details
Cognitive processes such as categorization and reliance on heuristics can produce prejudice by grouping individuals into categories and activating stereotypes, often with no deliberate malice (Devine, 1989). People use these cognitive shortcuts, for example, when favoring familiar groups—leading to biased judgments even in the absence of negative intent or emotion. This can be seen when social learning and mental efficiency drive the adoption of stereotypes automatically, rather than through conscious choice.

As Cicero wisely noted: "Now that we are rid of prejudice, now that time has modified it… your own sense of honour and justice has banished it from an examination of the facts—what case have I still to answer?" (Cicero, n.d.). Recognizing that prejudice can arise from neutral cognitive processes highlights the importance of awareness and critical thinking to address biases before they influence decisions and relationships.

An example of prejudice without malice can be observed when a hiring manager prefers applicants from their own alma mater, believing them to be more compatible simply due to shared experience, not out of animosity toward others. Recognizing these unintentional biases underscores the necessity of awareness and self-reflection to mitigate the impact of unconscious prejudices in daily decision-making (Devine, 1989). Cognitive contributions to prejudice demonstrate that intent is not always a factor—highlighting a need for thoughtful examination of automatic beliefs and behaviors before they shape outcomes.
