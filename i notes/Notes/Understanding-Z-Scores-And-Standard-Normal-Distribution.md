# Understanding Z-Scores and Standard Normal Distribution

## Summary
This note explains the standard normal distribution, z-scores, their sign, and numerical value in a way suitable for undergraduate students.

## Key Points
- **Standard Normal Distribution**: A standardized population distribution with mean 0 and standard deviation 1.
- **Sign of Z-Score**: Indicates whether a data point is above or below the mean.
- **Numerical Value of Z-Score**: Shows how many standard deviations away from the mean a data point is.

## Details
When a population distribution undergoes standardization, it produces what is known as the **standard normal distribution** or **z-distribution**. This standardized distribution has a mean of zero and a standard deviation of one, making it a fundamental tool in statistical analysis across various disciplines including psychology, business, and the social sciences.

The sign of a z-score provides critical directional information about where a data point falls relative to the distribution's mean. A positive z-score indicates that the data point lies above the mean, while a negative z-score signifies that the data point falls below the mean. For instance, if a student receives a z-score of +1.2 on an exam, this indicates performance above the class average, whereas a z-score of -0.8 would indicate performance below the class average. When a z-score equals zero, the raw score is exactly at the mean of the distribution.

The numerical value or magnitude of a z-score reveals the distance of a data point from the mean, expressed in standard deviation units. This magnitude tells us precisely how far—in terms of standard deviations—the observation deviates from the average. For example, a z-score of 2.0 indicates that the data point is located two standard deviations away from the mean, while a z-score of 0.5 indicates the data point is only half a standard deviation from the mean. The larger the absolute value of the z-score, the more extreme or unusual the observation is within the distribution.

Understanding both the sign and magnitude of z-scores allows researchers and practitioners to compare scores across different scales, identify outliers, and make probabilistic inferences about data points within a standardized framework.
