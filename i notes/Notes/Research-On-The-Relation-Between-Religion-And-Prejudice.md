# Research on the Relation Between Religion and Prejudice: Allport's Paradox Explained

## Summary
The role of religion in fostering or reducing prejudice is paradoxical, as described by Gordon Allport. This note explores this paradox from a scholarly perspective and draws parallels with Stoicism.

## Key Points
- **Religion as a Source of Prejudice:** Some religious orientations (extrinsic) are linked to higher levels of prejudice due to in-group/out-group thinking.
- **Religion as a Cure for Prejudice:** Intrinsic religious orientation fosters compassion, tolerance, and universal concern for humanity.
- **Empirical Support:** Studies show that intrinsic religiosity is associated with lower levels of prejudice.

## Details
Allport observed that religion can both heighten or reduce prejudice, depending on how it is practiced. This paradox is supported by empirical research using measures like the Religious Orientation Scale. Intrinsic religious orientation involves sincere, thoughtful self-reflection and values-driven choices, which can lead to more accepting attitudes toward others. Extrinsic religiosity, where religion serves as a tool for social advantage, tends to foster prejudice.

### Scholarly Perspective
- **Extrinsic Religiosity:** This orientation uses religion for personal gains such as belonging or status, leading to in-group/out-group thinking and higher levels of prejudice.
- **Intrinsic Religiosity:** This orientation sees religion as a deeply held guiding value, fostering compassion, tolerance, and universal concern. Religious teachings that emphasize love, humility, and justice can inspire prosocial behaviors.

### Parallels with Stoicism
Both intrinsic religious orientations and Stoicism offer pathways to reduced prejudice:
- **Universal Brotherhood:** Both teach the shared dignity of all people.
- **Self-Mastery and Reflection:** Encourage critical examination of judgments and desires, aiming to overcome passions that might lead to prejudice.
- **Transcendence of Particular Identities:** Focus on character and virtue rather than group membership.
