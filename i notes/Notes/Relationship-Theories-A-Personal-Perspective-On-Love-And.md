# Relationship Theories: A Personal Perspective on Love and Commitment

## Summary
This note explores the theoretical frameworks of attachment theory and the investment model, discussing their relevance to personal relationships. It highlights how these theories can explain emotional needs and commitment in partnerships.

## Key Points
- Attachment theory explains patterns established with caregivers influencing adult relationship expectations.
- The investment model focuses on satisfaction, perceived alternatives, and accumulated investments driving long-term commitment.
- Combining both frameworks provides a comprehensive understanding of attraction and relationship dynamics.

## Details
Attachment theory, first developed by John Bowlby and expanded by Mary Ainsworth, posits that patterns established with caregivers in childhood influence expectations and behavior in later relationships. The theory describes secure, anxious, avoidant, and disorganized attachment styles. Those with secure attachments tend to be more trusting, open, and able to form stable romantic bonds, whereas insecure attachment styles often result in anxiety or avoidance in adult partnerships (Ainsworth & Bell, 1970; Bowlby, 1969).

In personal experience, these patterns are evident in the way people—including myself—seek reassurance, react to conflict, and express needs. For example, having grown up in a supportive environment, it is easier to communicate openly and handle disagreements constructively in romantic relationships (Ainsworth & Bell, 1970). Friends who report anxious or avoidant tendencies often struggle with trust or fear of vulnerability, reflecting classic attachment theory pathways (Attachment Project, n.d.).

The investment model, developed by Caryl Rusbult, proposes that satisfaction, perceived quality of alternatives, and the size of investments (such as time, emotional energy, and shared memories) jointly determine commitment. This framework explains why many individuals stay in relationships—often even when other options may seem preferable—because they weigh losses associated with ending a partnership against the effort and emotional investment already made (Rusbult & Martens, 1987).

In practice, this is clear in long-term relationships: as shared experiences, mutual friends, and practical investments (homes, pets) accumulate, the commitment deepens, making the idea of separation more daunting. Observing couples who weather adversity—despite temporary dissatisfaction—further illustrates how investment can foster resilience and ongoing connection (Purdue University Libraries, n.d.).

## Why These Theories Stand Out
These frameworks complement each other: attachment theory explains emotional needs and responses, while the investment model reveals the underlying calculus of commitment. Both are reflected in personal and observed relationships, where early caregiving shapes interaction styles and accumulated investments drive decisions about staying or leaving (Rusbult & Martens, 1987).

For instance, someone with a secure attachment style may still experience relationship doubts during a rough patch, but the recognition of mutual investments—combined with emotional security—motivates working through issues, rather than seeking alternatives. Conversely, someone with an avoidant style might underestimate the value of relational investments, leading to premature withdrawal from otherwise promising connections (Ainsworth & Bell, 1970).

## Real-World Examples
- Open, honest conversations about feelings in a partnership reflect secure attachment (HelpGuide.org, n.d.).
- Deciding to work through a tough time because of shared living arrangements or years together reflects the investment model (Daniel Dashnaw Couple Therapy, n.d.).
- Friends who express anxiety over partner reliability or distances often demonstrate anxious attachment, sometimes persisting in unsatisfying relationships because of their high psychological or material investments (Ainsworth & Bell, 1970).
