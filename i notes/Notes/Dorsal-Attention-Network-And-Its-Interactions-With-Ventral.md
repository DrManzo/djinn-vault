# Dorsal Attention Network and Its Interactions with Ventral Attention and Subcortical Networks

## Summary
This note details the interactions between the dorsal attention network (DAN), ventral attention network (VAN), and subcortical structures in controlling cognitive focus. It explores how these networks dynamically interact to manage goal-directed and stimulus-driven attention, providing insights applicable to enhancing scriptural study and meditation.

## Key Points
- The **dorsal attention network** (DAN) is the brain's goal-directed attention system.
- The **ventral attention network** (VAN) functions as a stimulus-driven reorienting system.
- Subcortical structures like the superior colliculus, pulvinar, and caudate nucleus play crucial roles in modulating DAN activity.

## Details
The dorsal attention network (DAN), primarily comprising the intraparietal sulcus (IPS) and frontal eye fields (FEF), operates as the brain's "aperture," focusing cognitive resources on task-relevant information while filtering out distractions. This network maintains a dynamic, complementary relationship with the ventral attention network (VAN). The VAN includes the temporoparietal junction (TPJ) and inferior frontal gyrus, functioning as a stimulus-driven reorienting system that detects unexpected but behaviorally relevant events.

### Network Architecture and Core Functions
The DAN operates through top-down attentional control, maintaining focus on behaviorally relevant stimuli while biasing sensory processing areas to prioritize goal-relevant information. Distinct functional pathways exist within the DAN: a lateral projection connecting the FEF to IPS supports viewer-centered (egocentric) attention, while a medial pathway between the supplementary eye field (SEF) and superior parietal lobule (SPL1) enables more flexible, object-centered representations.

### Interactions with the Ventral Attention Network
The DAN and VAN interact through reciprocal inhibition and facilitation. During focused attention tasks like visual search, DAN activation is accompanied by VAN deactivation. The FEF inhibits the TPJ when no informative stimuli are present, filtering irrelevant distractions. However, when a behaviorally relevant unexpected stimulus appears, the VAN—particularly the right TPJ—generates a reorienting signal that temporarily suppresses DAN activity and redirects attention.

### Interactions with Subcortical Networks
The functionality of the DAN critically depends on subcortical structures including the superior colliculus (SC), pulvinar, head of caudate nucleus, and various brainstem nuclei. The SC receives direct retinal input and cortical projections from visual areas, creating topographic maps of visual space. Through ascending projections to the thalamus and basal ganglia, the SC modulates attention-related processing in the caudate nucleus.

The pulvinar participates in cortico-thalamocortical circuits that modulate information processing within the DAN. The head of caudate nucleus shows high statistical association with DAN functional connectivity maps and serves as a densely connected structural hub integrating attentional information. These subcortical nodes exhibit spatial correlation with acetylcholine nicotinic receptors and dopamine and serotonin transporters, revealing their role in neurochemical architecture.
