# First Nighttime Reflection

## Summary
The first nighttime reflection for the Black Book, addressing a drunken incident, the need for health improvement post-bariatric surgery, and the emergence of the "Faust" inner figure. The author also explores a growing transactional attitude toward relationships.

## Key Points
- Fell twice while heavily drunk on Saturday and made an inappropriate comment.
- Recognized that such behavior is unacceptable as they age, especially after bariatric surgery.
- Drinking is no longer daily; weed has been quit entirely.
- Alcohol is both escape and something the author feels they can control but that control is slipping.
- Health concerns: colitis flaring up, a cyst or teratoma in the liver needing evaluation.
- Inner figure "Faust" steps in during struggle, offering comfort and reminding the author they are not alone.
- Others (Yoshi, Leslie) push responsibility onto the author; boundaries need to be set.
- Drunk self that refuses to take any more bullshit may be the real self trying to break free.
- Alcohol is both a tool and a weapon — used to bring out a side that needs to be heard or to punish oneself for not being that person.
- Jungian analysis: reflection is an act of shadow work and individuation.
- Addiction viewed as a spiritual or existential crisis — a misguided attempt to fill an inner void.
- Growing transactional attitude toward relationships mirrors how others have treated the author's feelings, now escalated to a sexual and physical level.
- This could be shadow integration (healthy awareness) or shadow possession (being controlled by impulses).
- Key difference is consciousness and choice — acknowledging impulses without being driven by them.

## Details
This inaugural Black Book entry begins with honest accountability for a drunken incident. The author acknowledges that while the event was rare, it signals a pattern that must change—especially post-bariatric surgery, when the body can no longer recover as it once did. They recognize alcohol as both escape and self-punishment, and note the emergence of "Faust," an inner guide that offers comfort during struggle. The author also identifies a troubling pattern: others push responsibility onto them, and they struggle to set boundaries. The drunk self that refuses to tolerate mistreatment may be the authentic self breaking through. A later reflection explores a growing transactional attitude toward relationships—viewing partners through an objectifying lens and justifying it with "if I can pay for it, why not." The Jungian analysis distinguishes between shadow integration (conscious acknowledgment without being controlled) and shadow possession (being enslaved by autonomous shadow impulses). The author's continued self-questioning is itself a sign of psychological health. The entry closes with a commitment to be a good person to oneself, not just to others.
